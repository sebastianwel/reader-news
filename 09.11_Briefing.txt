Frederik Rönnow hält Union Berlin in Freiburg lange im Spiel. Doch seine Mitspieler lassen den Torhüter immer wieder im Stich. Nach der Niederlage schlägt der Däne Alarm.

Torhüter haben die undankbarste Position im Fußball. Sie sind Einzelkämpfer in einem Mannschaftssport. Das sieht man schon an der Trainersprache. 4-4-2, 3-5-2, 4-3-2-1 heißen die gängigen Formationen einer Startelf und wer das mal zusammenzählt, kommt nur auf zehn Spieler in der Mannschaft. Die Torhüter fehlen, als wären sie kein Teil des Teams. Wenn sie gerade nicht die Nummer eins sind, warten sie manchmal Monate auf der Bank auf ihren Moment. Wenn sie dann im Tor stehen, wird am meisten über ihre Fehler gesprochen. Torhüter spielen, um die besten Momente im Fußball zu verhindern. Und sie sind die Spieler auf dem Platz, die am meisten auf ihre Mitspieler angewiesen sind. Diese Erfahrung machte Union Berlins Frederik Rönnow in Freiburg.

Nach der 0:2-Niederlage beschrieb er im Interview mit DAZN eine seiner Heldentaten. Rönnow hatte einen wuchtigen Elfmeter gehalten, indem er mit einem Seitenschritt richtig spekulierte und kraftvoll in die Ecke hechtete. "Ich weiß, dass Grifo überragend schießt, da muss man dann ein bisschen früher gehen", so Rönnow. Und trocken hinterher: "Aber am Ende bringt's nichts."

Genau so war es am Sonntag. Rönnow ist zwar schon seit Jahren ein sicherer Rückhalt im Berliner Tor, aber in diesem Spiel zeigte er mal wieder, warum er zu den besten Torhütern in der ganzen Bundesliga gehört. [1] SCIENTISTS MAY HAVE DISCOVERED A MASSIVE STARLESS GALAXY

Some galaxies appear to contain all the ingredients needed to make stars, yet have failed to produce almost any.

Scientists have puzzled over why for years. Recent findings suggest that these “dark galaxies”—that is, galaxies that contain gas and dark matter but few or no stars—may be lurking throughout the cosmos. Finding them could help astronomers understand why some galaxies form stars while others remain dark, and test the best models of galaxy formation.

Researchers’ most successful model of the cosmos, known as Lambda-CDM, assumes the existence of two invisible ingredients: dark energy, the mysterious property thought to be driving the accelerating expansion of the universe, as well as cold dark matter, a hypothetical substance that physicists think makes up about 85 percent of the universe’s matter.

Clumps of dark matter, called haloes, provide the gravitational scaffolding for galaxies to form and grow, according to the model. Gas collects inside these haloes, eventually cooling and collapsing to form stars. But astronomers are still trying to understand why this process appears to happen in some haloes but not others.

One possibility is that some contain vast quantities of hydrogen gas, yet form very few stars. That would make them extremely difficult to spot.

Now, researchers have found a promising candidate for such a dark galaxy and published their findings in the Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society. The object, known as AGC 322753, contains roughly 2 billion times the sun's mass in hydrogen, yet appears to have no stars, though the researchers note that it is too big to be totally starless. However, if confirmed, it would be an unusually massive example of a starless galaxy.

Researchers at Swinburne University of Technology in Australia made the discovery by comparing observations from two existing radio surveys, one conducted by the Arecibo Observatory in Puerto Rico and the other by China's Five-Hundred-Meter Aperture Spherical Radio Telescope. Both telescopes had picked up a massive cloud of hydrogen.

Although other researchers have identified several dark galaxy candidates over the past decade, this one stands out for another reason: It may be rotating.

Hydrogen gas emits radio waves at a signature wavelength, which shortens when the gas moves toward Earth and stretches when it moves away. The signal from AGC 322753 shows two distinct peaks, suggesting that one side of the gas cloud is moving towards us while the other is receding.

This could mean that the gas has settled into an organized, rotating structure, much like the discs in ordinary galaxies, despite apparently having formed no stars.

"The theoretical models and the simulations show us that dark galaxies shouldn't rotate, so this is extremely interesting," says Marco Monaci, an author of the study at Swinburne University of Technology.

If AGC 322753 really is a rotating disc with no stars, astronomers will have to explain how the gas organized into such a structure without producing them.

That isn’t the only puzzle. Most models of galaxy formation predict that a cloud holding this much hydrogen should have formed at least a few stars, as its densest patches collapse under their own gravity and ignite.

The paper’s coauthors suggest it may be very unlikely that AGC 322753 contains absolutely no stars. So, instead, they think it may contain stars too faint to see–an “almost dark” galaxy. If so, they must be very faint indeed, because even deep optical images from the DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys, which search for starlight rather than the hydrogen gas detected by radio telescopes, reveal none.

From this, the team estimates that AGC322753 contains at least 40 times more mass in hydrogen than in stars, raising questions about what exactly suppressed stars from forming.

But further observations, such as ones made by the Very Large Array, a network of radio antennas in New Mexico, will be needed to confirm whether AGC 322573 is truly a dark—or almost dark—galaxy.

"Finding out more about it really is an essential next step at the moment. It's intriguing, and I want to know more," says Robert Minchin at the National Radio Astronomy Observatory.

Minchin knows how misleading such signals can be. He was involved in the discovery of VIRGOHI21, a dark galaxy candidate found in the early 2000s that was later reinterpreted as a long stream of hydrogen gas pulled from a nearby galaxy by gravity, known as a tidal tail.

Gas torn away like this can keep some of its original motion and mimic the signature of rotation, so Minchin says more observations are needed to rule out the same explanation for AGC 322753. Yet there is no obvious nearby galaxy it could have come from.

Rhys Taylor, an astronomer at the Astronomical Institute of the Czech Academy of Sciences in Prague, says tidal debris is unlikely to explain the observations. Reproducing the candidate dark galaxy’s distinctive double-peaked profile through such a process “is technically possible, but it’s fantastically unlikely,” Taylor says.

Taylor and Minchin both flag another concern, though. The two radio surveys that the researchers used placed the hydrogen at noticeably different positions in the sky. That suggests AGC 322753 may be an extended cloud of gas rather than a compact galaxy, another possibility that follow-up observations could help untangle.

Whether AGC 322753 turns out to be completely dark or merely exceptionally faint, understanding objects like it could still help astronomers work out why some galaxies form stars while others apparently do not, says Elisabeth Adams at the Planetary Science Institute.

One possible reason is a galaxy that spins so fast that its gas stays too spread out to collapse. Another is that ultraviolet light from the universe’s earliest stars heated the gas, keeping it too warm to condense. Studying such almost-dark galaxies could help tell these phenomena apart, revealing how the conditions of the early universe shaped the galaxies we see today.

“Once it’s no longer a dark galaxy, people seem to stop caring, and I actually think that’s not the right approach,” says Adams.

But if AGC 322753 truly is starless, it would be one of the most massive dark galaxies ever identified, potentially helping explain where the missing galaxies predicted by Lambda-CDM have been hiding. It could even help astronomers understand why some gas-rich galaxies form stars while others don't, providing a valuable test for models of galaxy formation—and perhaps even offering new clues about the nature of dark matter itself.

[2] AUDIOENGINE COLOR OF THE YEAR KIT REVIEW: BRIGHT LOOK, GOOD SOUND

The artist Wassily Kandinsky said, “Color is a power that directly influences the soul.” But in the realm of home stereo gear, the color wheel gets minimal airtime, with equipment typically available in scant options: silver-faced classics, wood-grain boxes, all manner of modern reflective piano black, and not a whole lot else.

Not so with Audioengine, which has painted a neat little world with its annual Color of the Year kit: a suite of home audio components, including laptop speakers, a subwoofer, a turntable, and speaker stands. Each year, the brand drops a new edition of the series in a different color; in the launch year of 2024, a dusky matte blue; in 2025, a subtle metallic green; and now in 2026, a lustrous and smooth egg-yolk yellow.

Having a home audio system in such an unusual color palette is, to me, irresistibly joyful; whenever I look at these stereo components, I can’t help but smile. And the tech holds up, too. After months of testing the suite, I was colored impressed. Straight out of the box, it's a great option for music lovers who value the look and sound of their home stereo setup.

The anchoring component of the Color of the Year suite are the A2+ Gen 3 Wireless Speakers. At 6 inches tall by 4 inches wide, they’re sized like laptop speakers—and get high marks for that purpose as WIRED’s top pick for computer speakers—but that’s really just the start of what they can do. Alone, the speakers pack enough power for a small home theater. They're also easy to integrate into a bigger system, thanks to their included stereo mini-jack inputs, USB inputs, and both stereo RCA inputs and outputs. To make the sound quality more targeted, place the A2+ speakers atop the included DS1M Desktop Stands, and angle them 15 degrees up toward your ears.

This year, Audioengine expanded its Color of the Year suite to include a color-matched Bluetooth turntable in collaboration with Crosley. Called the C6, it's a manual two-speed machine with a nice, heavy steel platter and medium-density fiberboard (MDF) plinth. It's Bluetooth-enabled and features a built-in preamp that lets you directly connect your A2+ speakers—wired or wireless—without a need for additional components.

My favorite part of the Color of the Year suite is the S6 Powered Subwoofer. At 10 inches by 8.7 inches, the compact, front-firing form factor lets you snuggle it into various spaces that wouldn't work for a larger, bottom-firing subwoofer. This is a class D sub with a frequency response down to 33 Hz and an adjustable crossover down to 40 Hz that helps your system sound complete, lifting the bass and rounding out the listening experience.

The ease of setup is one of the major draws for the Audioengine suite. Plug in the A2+ speakers, link them together using the provided stereo cables, and turn them on. You can connect the speakers via Bluetooth, or plug in other components via the stereo inputs. It’s as basic as home hi-fi gets.

Linking the Crosley C6 turntable using Bluetooth is similarly seamless. The A2+s have an intuitive Bluetooth pairing mode that starts up when you turn on the speakers, indicated by a flashing Pair button on the back. From there, turn on the turntable, make sure it’s in the “line” position (by using the turntable’s built-in preamp), and the C6 instantly searches for the A2+s. Once the two units connect, the flashing button on the back of the speakers will turn blue, as will the Bluetooth indicator on the turntable.

Connecting the subwoofer was likewise a cinch: Connect an RCA cable to the back of the A2+s in the RCA output, then run that to the audio input on the back of the S6 subwoofer. Once you connect, you’ll want to tweak the sound and presence of the subwoofer, and once you’re happy with the dial-in, the subwoofers will go up and down in volume, in lockstep with the A2+ speakers.

Each Audioengine product also came with a QR code that launched a step-by-step setup process, which I found especially helpful for dialing in the sound for the woofer. (The Audioengine YouTube channel is also a wealth of information.)

After several colorful, music-filled months of testing the Audioengine kit, I found the system to be versatile, though not without its limitations or opportunities for upgrades and tweaks. Right out of the box, the speakers, subwoofer, and turntable looked fantastic and sounded pretty nice. The A2+ speakers were shockingly nuanced and detailed, considering their small size, with Primus and Frank Zappa records showcasing slashing guitars and Paul Chambers and Bill Evans jazz albums providing a sophisticated soundstage. These speakers also worked great for home gaming and laptop use. The subwoofer did wonders when connected to the A2+ for elevating the home-theater experience. The growling lion and car-chasing monkeys in 1995's Jumanji were vivid and lifelike.

Being able to transition from home stereo to computer to home theater is versatile and fun. It means there’s always a way to incorporate these products into your home.

If there’s a weak link in the Color of the Year kit, it’s the turntable—but even that is pretty strong. The aluminum tonearm leaves something to be desired if you’re accustomed to higher-end units, but the C6 worked well for me and will suit the needs of most casual listeners. There are several similarly priced Bluetooth-enabled turntables on the market, so out of curiosity I swapped in a Sony PS-LX3BT and noticed no significant difference in terms of sound. What those other turntables lack is the aesthetic unity and the satisfaction of having your entire system kitted out in a unique colorway.

Mostly, I just love how this canary-yellow gear looks together. It offers equal parts interior-design pleasure and home audio performance. The price is incredibly competitive across the entry-level home stereo space, and the ease of use and setup feels intuitive and user-friendly for novices. If color influences the soul, then the union of color and music stands to be a source of deep satisfaction—and I’ve found it here.

(If you don't want the entire suite, you can buy the components à la carte without a penalty—there's no price break for the bundled suite.)

[3] AUTHORS CAN DEFEND AGAINST AI ALLEGATIONS WITH ‘PAPER TRAIL’ TOOLS. IS IT ENOUGH?

Andrea Bartz is a prolific novelist with an impressive résumé. Since 2019, she has published five thrillers, become a New York Times bestselling author, had her work optioned by the likes of Netflix and Hulu, and picked up an endorsement from Reese Witherspoon’s influential book club.

She also has a legal claim to fame: Bartz was the lead plaintiff in a class-action suit against Anthropic that alleged the company had downloaded millions of copyrighted books from pirate sites to use as training data for its models. Anthropic last year agreed to pay $1.5 billion in the largest copyright settlement in US history.

But while Bartz and many other writers were on guard against this plunder of their work, another AI threat was brewing. What happens when authors are accused of relying on artificial intelligence themselves?

This past March, the publishing industry found out: Following widespread speculation on social media that a horror novel titled Shy Girl was largely AI-generated, and a Times article about those claims, Hachette Book Group canceled an upcoming US publication run for the novel and discontinued it in the UK, where it had already been released. According to Pangram, a robust but by no means infallible AI detection tool, some 78 percent of the text was AI-generated.

Mia Ballard, the author of Shy Girl, has denied allegations that she used AI to write the novel, claiming that an editor was responsible for AI-assisted changes to a previously self-published version of the book. She has also said that she is pursuing legal action. Since that scandal broke, several more fiction authors have run afoul of AI suspicions, one after winning a short story prize, another after publishers bid into the millions for his crime novel. Both have denied these allegations as well.

But Bartz notes that once the accusations start, the damage is done. As the Shy Girl scandal erupted, she says, “I kind of immediately felt like this was going to lead to a potential witch hunt.” The unavoidable emergence of AI in publishing, she explains, will “have the unintended effect of leading anyone to be able to accuse anyone of using AI,” and at the moment, authors have little recourse in defending themselves against these charges. “There’s no agreed upon standard,” Bartz says, in proving human authorship.

The developers of a host of new software tools hope to change that. Platforms including Workings, Blindproof, Wintertext, Human Ink, and ObraVera promise the means to track and document an author’s writing process, in some cases encrypting and fingerprinting the written work or typing rhythms with unique codes, in order to produce a record of authorship (whether entirely human or AI-assisted). The idea is not just to create a paper trail that writers can point to if someone questions whether they used an LLM but to demonstrate provenance when, for example, text is scraped into AI training sets without attribution or other identifying metadata.

Mary Rasenberger, CEO of the Authors Guild, a professional organization focused on free expression and copyright protection, says that the guild can’t recommend any one of these tools over another but wants writers to know they exist as something of a security net. (Many of the companies have approached the Authors Guild to pitch their services, according to Umair Kazi, the guild’s director of policy and advocacy.)

At the same time, Rasenberger tells WIRED, it’s “insulting” and a “burden” to have to jump through this hoop, and “we don't feel it should be required” for authors to preserve receipts.

“If what you're selling are your words and your voice and your imagination, your creativity, using AI doesn't make sense,” Rasenberger says, noting that authors’ pride in their own work means AI slop is far less common in publishing than one might think. “I've talked to editors, particularly editors of literary nonfiction and fiction,” Rasenberger says, “and some of them will say, ‘I never see AI-generated submissions, I'd know it if I did.’”

Rasenberger worries that authors are up against a climate of distrust—they should “refrain from accusations of other authors because it just does not support a healthy literary community,” she says—and overconfidence in AI detection tools, which can be fooled and have been known to produce false positives. While Pangram claims an “industry-leading” false positive rate of 0.01 percent when used on creative writing, Rasenberger said the Authors Guild has “heard of lots of instances where people have put their own human-authored work in Pangram and gotten a result back saying it was AI-generated.” There are a variety of factors, she adds, that can “make an AI detector think something's AI-generated when it's not.”

By the same token, a process-tracking app isn’t foolproof; the AI arms race means there’s an ongoing cycle of vulnerabilities and fixes on both sides.

“We offer a product that [tracks writing] in Google Docs, and many users find it helpful,” says Max Spero, cofounder and CEO of Pangram. “With that said, ‘paper trail’ tools are not a perfect catch-all. For example, someone could hand-type AI text, or use an auto-typer.” Spero points out that the messiness of writing itself creates further problems. “The average writer doesn't just sit down and type out a book,” he says. “They work with multiple files, send drafts back and forth, use voice notes while on the go. Not all of this gets captured in writing process tools.”

The tools, meanwhile, may be more geared toward copyright concerns than confirmation of human or AI authorship. Brady Ridgway, technical founder of ObraVera—which can cryptographically “fingerprint” patterns in writing as well as audio voice recordings—says his platform is designed to trace reuse of material you have stored in their database as a registered human user. With a time stamp and a crypto hash, he explains, if the content “crops up later somewhere it's not supposed to be, or somebody else claims it as theirs, we can search the database and say, ‘These words were created at this time, by this person.’”

That may demonstrate provenance and ownership, Ridgway says, yet it’s up to the registered individual to declare whether they used AI or not in the creation of the text. He refers to the kerfuffle in May over the writer who was accused of winning a Commonwealth Short Story Prize with an AI-generated submission. “Everybody seems to agree that he's the person who submitted the short story,” Ridgway says. “That's all that the software would do. It would say that somebody on this day made a declaration that it was human only or not AI-assisted.” It can’t confirm whether that’s a true statement or not.

Some argue that trying to institutionalize vetting systems for human authorship is simply impractical—and may soon be a moot point anyway. Jane Friedman, an author and publishing business expert, recently argued in her industry newsletter The Bottom Line, that the era of AI scandals is likely to be short-lived, given the improvements of Pangram and similar detectors. Moreover, she wrote, authors have now seen what may happen if they attempt to pass off AI-generated writing as their own.

Friedman tells WIRED that agents and publishers are unlikely to implement any method of verifying human authorship that “costs extra money or extra time,” not least because it would be impossible to get them to commit to a shared standard. She predicts that “it will be like herding cats to get agreement on this, because publishing is slow to adopt new technologies, especially new technologies developed by industry outsiders.”

Even if diverse publishers with highly variable needs and resources were to coalesce around some theoretical form of authentication, hurdles remain. Friedman alludes to the logistical problems of integrating this benchmark into existing production models, not to mention the lingering problem of “faked or manipulated” certification. “If any of this costs extra money or extra time, forget it,” she concludes. And while authors may be willing to pay for peace of mind, Friedman says, they won’t be able to trust that agents and publishers will accept proof of authorship from any given app.

In the absence of a clear solution, writers are left to fend for themselves. Bartz says that she’s alarmed by how zealous readers are responding to the prospect of being deceived by AI. “I have heard of people getting review-bombed on Goodreads because some stranger felt that the book sounded like AI to them, and everyone else piled on,” she says. “I've seen readers saying, ‘I'm not gonna read anything published after 2022, because then I can be sure that it doesn't have generative AI in it.’ That's a great way to tank an entire industry all at once.”

It so happens that Bartz’s forthcoming 2027 book, Delete Your Account, tackles generative artificial intelligence head-on. Her protagonist is a blocked writer who opts for an unfortunate shortcut, prompting an AI chatbot to imitate her favorite thriller—only to find herself trapped in that original story, facing certain death.

Bartz wonders if the very choice to satirize AI has put her in a precarious spot. “I've written portions that are very much meant to sound like LLMs,” she says. “Transcripts I made up, and annoying, AI-sounding marketing materials from this made-up AI company. And I don't know—if any of that comes back as AI-written, what will I do?”
