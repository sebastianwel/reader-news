[1] "NATIONALES INTERESSE" - TRUMP SETZT GEPLANTE SANKTIONEN GEGEN RUSSLAND AUS

Erst macht der US-Präsident einen umstrittenen Diesel-Deal mit Russland, nun setzt Trump gerade erst beschlossene Sanktionen gegen Moskau aus. Dies liege im nationalen Interesse, teilte das Weiße Haus mit, ohne dies näher zu begründen.

Wenige Tage nach seinem Diesel-Abkommen mit Moskau hat US-Präsident Donald Trump nun überraschend ein erst kürzlich verabschiedetes Sanktionspaket gegen Russland auf Eis gelegt. Die vom US-Kongress beschlossenen drastischen Strafmaßnahmen ruhen damit bis auf Weiteres.

Trump begründete sein Vorgehen damit, dass dies "zum jetzigen Zeitpunkt im nationalen Interesse" der USA liege. Das Weiße Haus machte bisher keine Angaben darüber, inwiefern die Aussetzung der Sanktionen im nationalen Interesse liege.

Erst Mitte September hatte Trump das vom US-Parlament verabschiedete Gesetz mit neuen weitreichenden Strafmaßnahmen gegen Russland unterzeichnet. Es richtete sich insbesondere gegen den russischen Energiesektor.

Trump setzte die Zölle nun per Dekret kurz vor Ende einer vom Kongress vorgegebenen Frist aus. Das Gesetz sah vor, dass die im Text verankerten Zölle und Sanktionen 30 Tage nach der Unterzeichnung automatisch greifen - es sei denn, der Präsident würde innerhalb dieser Zeit anders entscheiden.

Im US-Kongress regt sich Widerstand gegen den von Präsident Trump angekündigten Import von russischem Diesel.

Der Kongress wollte mit den Sanktionen den Druck auf Russland erhöhen, angesichts gestiegener Treibstoffpreise setzte der wegen der Zwischenwahlen unter Druck stehende Trump jedoch andere Prioritäten.

Vor wenigen Tagen hatte er ungeachtet bestehender Sanktionen mit dem Kreml die Lieferung von russischem Diesel vereinbart und danach den Druck auf die Ukraine und Präsident Wolodymyr Selenskyj verstärkt.

Trump bestritt nun aber, dass er für die vorübergehende Drosselung des militärisch genutzten US-Satelliteninternets von Starlink in der Ukraine verantwortlich gewesen sei. "Das habe ich nicht getan. Nein, wir haben das überhaupt nicht getan", sagte er vor Reportern in Washington.

Trumps Aussagen stehen im Widerspruch zu jenen von Elon Musk, dem der Konzern SpaceX, der Starlink betreibt, gehört. "Jegliche Geschwindigkeits- oder Nutzungsbeschränkungen werden vom US-Militär und letztlich vom US-Präsidenten festgelegt", hatte dieser zuvor auf X gepostet.

[2] NACHFOLGE ALS UN-GENERALSEKRETÄR: BEWERBER SCHEITERN AN VETOMÄCHTEN

Bis Ende Dezember muss der UN-Sicherheitsrat eine Nachfolge für Generalsekretär Guterres bestimmen. Doch auch bei der sechsten Probeabstimmung fielen alle Kandidaten bei den Vetomächten durch.

Die bisherige Favoritin für das Amt des UN-Generalsekretärs, die frühere Vizepräsidentin von Costa Rica, Rebeca Grynspan, darf aber weiter hoffen. Bei der sechsten Probeabstimmung im UN-Sicherheitsrat lag die 70-Jährige erneut an der Spitze des Bewerberfeldes.

Für Grynspan sprachen sich 9 der 15 Mitgliedsstaaten aus. Ein Staat enthielt sich. Fünf Staaten stimmten gegen sie, darunter auch zwei Vetomächte. Dies wurde dadurch sichtbar, weil die fünf Vetomächte erstmals mit roten Stimmzetteln wählten.

Da die Abstimmung jedoch geheim ist, blieb unklar, wer die beiden ablehnenden Vetomächte sind. UN-Beobachter gehen davon aus, dass es sich wahrscheinlich um Russland und die USA handelt.

Zwei Männer und vier Frauen kämpfen um die Nachfolge für das Amt des UN-Generalsekretärs.

Um UN-Generalsekretär zu werden, benötigt man nicht nur die Zustimmung von mindestens 9 der 15 Mitgliedsstaaten. Zusätzlich darf keine der fünf Vetomächte mit Nein stimmen.

UN-Beobachter gehen nun von zwei Szenarien aus: Entweder gelingt es Grynspan, in weiteren Gesprächen die noch ablehnenden Vetomächte umzustimmen. Oder die Vetomächte präsentieren in den nächsten Wochen einen ganz neuen Kandidaten.

Damit wäre allerdings das bisherige transparente Auswahlverfahren gescheitert, das die frühere Präsidentin der UN-Generalversammlung Annalena Baerbock angestoßen hatte, um das bislang übliche Auskungeln der Spitzenpersonalie unter den Vetomächten zu beenden.

[3] GKV-VERBANDSCHEF FORDERT BESSERSTELLUNG GESETZLICH VERSICHERTER

Dass gesetzlich Versicherte oft bei der Terminvergabe benachteiligt würden, hält der Chef des GKV-Spitzenverbands, Blatt, für eine "himmelschreiende Ungerechtigkeit". Stattdessen solle künftig der medizinische Bedarf entscheiden.

Gesetzlich Versicherte müssen nach Ansicht des Spitzenverbandes der gesetzlichen Krankenkassen (GKV) bei der Vergabe von Arztterminen bessergestellt werden. "Wir müssen den Zugang zur ambulanten Versorgung so organisieren, dass Termine nach medizinischem Bedarf vergeben werden - und nicht danach, wie jemand versichert ist", sagte Verbandschef Oliver Blatt den Funke-Medien.

75 Millionen Menschen seien gesetzlich versichert und finanzierten den allergrößten Teil der ärztlichen Honorare. Trotzdem würden sie bei der Terminvergabe häufig benachteiligt. Das sei eine "himmelschreiende Ungerechtigkeit".

Mit der Reform der gesetzlichen Krankenversicherung will die Regierung die Ausgaben im Gesundheitssystem bremsen.

Den Begriff "Zwei-Klassen-Medizin" lehne er jedoch ab, so Blatt. Im Krankenhaus und von den meisten Ärzten würden gesetzlich Versicherte genauso gut behandelt wie Privatpatienten. Die Ungleichbehandlung zeige sich vor allem bei den Wartezeiten. "Ärzte sollen weiterhin Privatpatienten behandeln, aber nicht automatisch bevorzugen", forderte der GKV-Chef.

Bundeskanzler Friedrich Merz hatte im September eine Debatte über das bestehende duale System aus gesetzlicher und privater Krankenversicherung angestoßen. Er sprach von einem Gerechtigkeitsproblem, wenn sich der Eindruck verfestige, dass Privatpatienten bevorzugt behandelt würden - und stellte Änderungen in Aussicht.

[1] GEISTESBLITZ ASEKO, TOR EBNOUTALIB - FRANKFURT BEZWINGT KÖLN

Lange mühte sich Eintracht Frankfurt in der Fußball-Bundesliga gegen einen harmlosen 1. FC Köln. Doch dann griff Noel Aseko ganz tief in seine Trick-Kiste und bereitete das Siegtor durch Younes Ebnoutalib wunderbar vor (77. Minute).

Durch das 1:0 (0:0) am Freitagabend springt die Eintracht an den Kölnern vorbei und kommt nach sechs Spielen auf acht Zähler, Köln verpasste durch die Niederlage den theoretisch möglichen Sprung auf Rang fünf und hängt in der unteren Tabellenhälfte fest.

Im Vergleich zum Derbysieg gegen Borussia Mönchengladbach hatte Kölns Trainer René Wagner seine Startelf auf zwei Positionen verändert: Der verletzte Ellyes Skhiri und Mikey Moore (Bank) rotierten raus, Joel Schmied und Tom Krauß begannen. Das bedeutete auch eine taktische Umstellung, die Kölner agierten mit Dreier- beziehungsweise Fünferkette.

Wagners Gegenüber Adi Hütter setzte nach der zuletzt desolaten Leistung gegen RB Leipzig auf drei Neue: Mario Götze, Fares Chaibi und Ebnoutalib begannen für Can Uzun, Ansgar Knauff und Noel Aseko. Der in die Kritik geratene Kapitän Robin Koch durfte wie schon von Hütter angekündigt wieder von Anfang an auflaufen.

Beinahe hätte die Eintracht wie schon beim 0:6 gegen RB unmittelbar nach Anpfiff direkt den ersten Treffer kassiert, doch Noah Atubolu parierte gegen den links nach einem Stellungsfehler von Nnamdi Collins enteilten Marius Bülter (1.). Die Gastgeber arbeiteten sich rein in diese Begegnung: Chaibi scheiterte in der achten Minute nach einem guten Dribbling aus spitzem Winkel an FC-Keeper Marvin Schwäbe.

Danach beruhigte sich die Begegnung ein wenig und die Frankfurter bekamen nach und nach mehr Kontrolle, ohne daraus viel Druck zu kreieren. In der 21. Minute kam Chaibi mal nach einem langem Ball erneut links im Strafraum zum Abschluss, aber wieder war der Winkel gegen Schwäbe zu spitz. Nach einer halben Stunde musste auf der anderen Seite Atubolu sein ganzes Können aufbieten: Eine Freistoßflanke von links verlängerte Bülter mit dem langen Bein aufs kurze Eck, aber der Frankfurter Schlussmann reagierte glänzend.

Sekunden vor dem Pausenpfiff schloss Said El Mala noch einen Sprint über das ganze Feld mit seinem Signature-Schuss auf die lange Ecke ab - dachte man. Doch die Kugel rutschte dem Nationalspieler über den Spann und landete weit neben dem Kasten. Ein insgesamt fußballerisch ziemlich mauer erster Durchgang endete folgerichtig 0:0.

Hälfte zwei begann mit einem Ebnoutalib-Kopfball, der knapp rechts am Kölner Tor vorbeistrich (48.), die Gastgeber waren besser aus der Kabine gekommen und kamen nun auch häufiger in Strafraumnähe. Eine Flanke nach der anderen segelte jetzt in den Kölner Sechzehner, Schwäbe parierte einen Kopfball von Lilian Brassier (54.), dann noch einen von Chaibi (58.), der eigentlich die Führung hätte bedeuten müssen. Der aufmerksame Kölner Torwart musste dabei zusehen, wie seine Vorderleute gar nicht mehr zu Entlastung kamen.

Um das zu ändern, brachte Wagner Moore und Paul Okon-Engstler für Isak Johannesson und Linton Maina, sowie Alessio Castro-Montes für den angeschlagenen Schmied. Auf der anderen Seite musste Hütter Collins durch Ansgar Knauff ersetzen, nachdem Collins sich am linken Bein verletzt hatte. Außerdem kam Aseko für Götze. An der Dynamik dieser zerfahrenen Partie änderten auch all diese Wechsel erst einmal nichts: Die Eintracht war besser, aber außer nach Flanken nicht gefährlich und Köln vor allem mit Verteidigen beschäftigt.

Mit dem besten Angriff des ganzen Spiels gingen die Frankfurter schließlich doch noch in Führung. Und das nicht nach einer Flanke: Ebnoutalib bediente rechts am Strafraumrand Aseko und startete in Richtung Tor, Aseko nahm den Ball wunderbar mit der Sohle mit und steckte in der gleichen Bewegung durch die Beine von Luka Lochoshvili wieder durch zu Ebnoutalib, der vor Schwäbe eiskalt ins kurze Eck abschloss (77.).

Die Gäste mühten sich in der Schlussphase, um irgendwie noch zum glücklichen Ausgleich zu kommen, blieben dabei aber zu harmlos und mussten am Ende eine verdiente Auswärtsniederlage hinnehmen. "Ich finde wir hätten uns für die aktive und positive erste Hälfte belohnen müssen", meinte Kölns Schwäbe nachher am Sportschau-Mikro und fand die Niederlage "extremst bitter". Die Eintracht bejubelt einen kleinen Befreiungsschlag und bleibt erstmals in dieser Saison ohne Gegentor. Ein erleichterter Siegtorschütze Ebnoutalib sagte: "Es war eine schwierige Woche. Mental war das sehr, sehr viel." Und ergänzte: "Wir wollten eine Reaktion zeigen und das haben wir auch gemacht."

Frankfurt ist am kommenden Samstagabend zum Topspiel bei Borussia Dortmund zu Gast (18.30 Uhr), Köln empfängt zuvor den FC Schalke 04 (15.30 Uhr).

[1] THE LEAP SECOND IS NO MORE

Leap seconds have driven a raging debate in the metrological community for three decades. Now, that argument has been put to rest: The leap second is officially dead.

Doing away with an obscure metric the average person has been blissfully unaware of may seem like a novelty, but killing the leap second has major ramifications for technology you rely on every day. It also helps stave off what could’ve been a dangerous moment in time: The possibility of the world’s first negative leap second.

The International Bureau of Weights and Measurements (known by its French acronym BIPM) on Thursday said that in May 2027 it would stop issuing leap seconds, 32 of which have been added since 1972 to adjust what’s known as Coordinated Universal Time (UTC).

The world implemented the leap second in 1972 due to a growing gap between time measured with atomic clocks and time based on the Earth’s rotation. The reason for the discrepancy? The latter is slowing down due in large part to tidal friction caused by the moon and Earth’s rotation not lining up. The molten core of the Earth as well as oceans and the atmosphere also affect the spin rate.

Leap seconds are a way to keep atomic-clock-driven UTC in line with astronomically kept Universal Time (UT1). Think of it like adding a little extra track for a train to keep running exactly parallel with another one. Rather than happening on a set interval—like a leap year—BIPM uses the average of hundreds of atomic clocks around the world and compares their time calculations to astronomical time to determine when a leap second is necessary. The most recent one was added in 2016.

Of course, adding track is no small effort, and it may seem like a lot of hassle for a measly second. But UTC is vital for all sorts of technology given the number of APIs and databases that rely on it. Introducing a leap second helped keep UTC and UT1 aligned, but it also wrought havoc on various systems running on UTC and forced companies to figure out how to deal with them when they were added every few years.

A number of tech companies like Google and Meta use “a ‘smear’ method of dealing with leap seconds,” Judah Levine, a fellow at the US National Institute of Standards and Technology, writes in an email. In essence, they spread a leap second out over the course of a day or so in tiny increments. But leap seconds have thrown off other systems; the airline Qantas, for example, saw its booking system borked by a leap second in 2012.

Adding a leap second is hard enough as it is. But the odds of having to add a negative leap second have been rising in part because Earth has picked up rotational speed in recent years due to climate change. (There’s always a climate angle.) Melting ice has caused a shift in the planet’s gravity so extreme that a negative leap second may have been required as soon as 2029, according to a 2024 Nature study. Part of the calculus of ditching the leap second altogether was that going negative isn’t worth the risk.

“There are continuing problems with positive leap seconds even after 50 years, and the concern was that the negative leap second would be much more serious because its effects had never been tested,” Levine says.

BIPM time department director Patrizia Tavella confirmed those concerns in an email to WIRED. “The probability of a negative leap second convinced us [to] set the application date in 2027,” she wrote, adding that there was added urgency following tests by an industry standards group that “showed several failures with a negative leap.”

Tavella says the group is waiting until May 20 2027 to implement it because it's the anniversary of 1875 signing of the Metre Convention, creating BIPM, as well as Metrology Day. In other words, it’s the perfect time to mark the passing of time—or more accurately, the lack thereof.

The death of the leap second makes life easier for everyone now. “The nice fact is that we have nothing to do!” Tavella says. “Just continue to accumulate second[s] and ‘normal’ minutes of 60 seconds.”

Levine concurs, writing that “life will have one less hassle.”

But kicking the can on adding leap seconds doesn’t mean atomic time and astronomical time won’t continue to drift apart. That’s why BIPM also agreed to create a leap hour. Given how long it takes leap seconds to add up, a leap hour is millennia away. (There are, after all, 3,600 seconds in an hour.)

“We do not speak of leap hour yet,” Tavella says. “If systems rely on UTC, they have nothing to do. If they rely on UT1, they have to be able to use the information UT1-UTC which, in [the] future, will be larger than 1 second.”

In other words, not our problem, and good luck to future generations!

[2] KATIE ZACHARIA WAS LINKED TO AN AI RARE-EARTHS STARTUP. IT’S EVEN WEIRDER THAN IT SOUNDS

Before being fired five days into her stint as White House press secretary, Katie Zacharia spent much of the day attempting to explain her past.

Throughout her brief tenure, Zacharia’s résumé was the source of much intrigue. Foremost was the fact that despite claiming to be an attorney for much of her career, she never actually passed the California bar, as she was eventually forced to admit. Another element of her professional history, WIRED reported, was a role as an affiliate marketer for Beachbody, a fitness multilevel marketing company.

WIRED has now uncovered new details of her relationship to an AI-powered rare-earth mining operation called NRED Intelligent Mining, which counts former Department of Homeland Security secretary Kristi Noem as a strategic adviser.

On June 24, NRED—then known as NovaRed Mining, a small copper-and-gold exploration company in Vancouver, Canada—issued a press release to share that Zacharia, who had left a role as a DHS spokesperson two months before, was joining the advisory board alongside Noem.

In her new role, according to the release, Zacharia would provide “strategic guidance” to NovaRed’s board of directors as it advanced exploration projects, as well as “emerging artificial intelligence initiatives.”

It does not appear, though, that she ever worked there. When asked on Friday whether she was still on the board, she said, “That was fake,” and referred WIRED to Sam Amsterdam, a “strategic marketing and media engagement professional” whose clients have included the government of Kazakhstan and political parties in Georgia, Nigeria, and Zimbabwe.

“That release was not approved by Ms. Zacharia and was issued without her consent,” says Amsterdam, whose brother is listed as a member of NRED management on the company’s website and said he was speaking as a friend and colleague of Zacharia, not a representative. “I know all that to be factual.” He quickly added that while there had been discussions of her joining the company, she hadn’t actually done so.

Valentin Saitarli, who was NRED’s chief technology officer and says he is onboarding as CEO, confirmed that there were discussions about a role but said that it “never materialized,” adding, “It was a bit like signing up for a gym but never actually going for a workout.” Describing NRED, Saitarli says, “We go full robots and AI.”

Despite Amsterdam’s understanding that it had been withdrawn, the press release claiming Zacharia was joining the company remained on NRED’s website as of Friday. An archived version of the website showed Zacharia listed as management as of at least July.

When WIRED asked Saitarli about this, he said that the site was being updated.

Zacharia, whose NovaRed role was announced eight days after Noem’s appointment was announced, worked under Noem as DHS’s deputy assistant press secretary for public affairs for about a month and was reported to have close ties with de facto DHS chief of staff Corey Lewandowski. (She denied any such ties to The Daily Mail.)

The announcement that Noem was joining the advisory board of a Canadian mining company triggered an outcry among Canadians, who pointed to statements she had made referring to Canada as the “51st state.” At that time, The Globe and Mail described NovaRed as “a copper and gold exploration company that generates no revenue.” The paper noted that in January, NoraRed was holding only $65,689 in cash, which the company said “wouldn’t be enough to cover all of its expected expenses for the next 12 months.”

But around the spring, something changed. NovaRed began stacking its advisory board not with geologists and technical consultants—which would be typical for a junior mining company—but with heavy hitters with ties to the defense sector, MAGA, and other international entities.

Earlier this month, NRED announced that it was pivoting away from its flagship Wilmac Copper-Gold Project and is putting all its resources into developing and commercializing “MetalCore,” its “AI-assisted mineral exploration platform.”

“I want to build a new generation of robotics and mining,” Saitarli tells WIRED. “Do we want to get stuck in the Stone Age or horses age? No, we want to live on Mars, spend time in hotels in space, have a wedding party on the moon … We will bring robotics and AI to global mining and beyond our planet, hopefully.”

Phil Ehr, a retired US Navy commander who is running for Congress as a Democrat in Florida, was brought into NovaRed earlier this year. “I know nothing about mining. I know nothing about commodities,” Ehr tells WIRED in a phone call. “But I do know that we need more copper. I do know that we need more critical minerals.” He had only been at the company for a couple of months when, he said, he was blindsided by NovaRed’s announcement that Kristi Noem and “another DHS spokesperson”—whom he recalls being Zacharia—had also been brought onto the advisory board.

“To the best of my knowledge, [Zacharia] came at the same time as Kristi Noem did without any consultation to me and was just like, boom, there,” recalls Ehr.

Noem, who did not immediately reply to requests for comment, was fresh off overseeing the disastrous ICE occupation in Minneapolis, and Ehr didn’t want to be associated with her. “So I asked the company, “Why is Kristi Noem here? I just can't work with her because she is so controversial. We are on different sides of what's right and wrong in, in the federal government,” says Ehr.

He thought it was odd that NovaRed seemed to be stacking its ranks with people who, like him, didn’t know very much about the mining industry. He expected some geologists in the mix, or technical consultants. Instead, some of the other people hired included Gregory Fedun, who previously “served as a key advisor to the United Arab Emirates’ Al Mualla Royal Family on a range of international projects,” and media executive Lee Caplin, coproducer of HBO’s True Detective series. Ehr resigned from the advisory board not long after Noem joined.

While she was attempting to clarify the nature of her relationship to NRED, Zacharia was also tending to other matters. On Thursday evening, while she still held her position as White House press secretary, Zacharia reached out to a WIRED reporter to suggest they contact fitness influencer named Jenelle Summers for “clarity” on Beachbody, the MLM she was previously affiliated with and which Summer brought her into.

Summers said she didn’t recall much about Zacharia—whom, she said, she met online—other than that “she was always kind to me and someone that was into fitness and believed in the products and programs.”

“To my recollections,” she added, “she didn't do much with it.” Summers rejected the idea that she had been involved in a “pyramid scheme,” which is how Zacharia had characterized the company to WIRED. ”That's kind of old-school thinking,” Summers said. “It was a network marketing business model.”

“I don't have any comment on that other than to say she's always been kind to me,” Summers said when asked what she made of Zacharia’s rise to prominence.

Zacharia did not respond to requests for comment on her firing. When asked if he had comment on her having been let go, Amsterdam told a WIRED reporter, “Well, that’s news to me!”

[3] CHICAGO-AREA COPS HELPED HSI SPY ON MINNESOTA ANTI-ICE ACTIVISTS

Chicago-area law enforcement officers took part in an undercover probe by Immigration and Customs Enforcement into Minneapolis mutual aid networks last spring as part of the feds’ conspiracy investigation into 15 Twin Cities activists facing federal felony charges for resisting Operation Metro Surge, according to newly released documents reviewed by WIRED.

An April report by ICE’s investigations wing, Homeland Security Investigations, titled “Antifa Roadshow,” details in-person and electronic surveillance by a joint task force of an April 2 talk. The task force was made up of 14 law enforcement officers—six local cops and eight HSI Chicago agents. The talk included self-described anarchists in Minnesota who spoke about their organizing experiences during the three-month immigration surge, which resulted in the killings of two US citizens.

The document, released by the 15 Minnesota activists’ defense team as part of the ongoing federal case against them, exposes close cooperation between local police, sheriffs, and HSI agents on a prosecution that critics say crosses the line into political repression. The Chicago HSI task force, which included officers from Chicago, Aurora, Downer’s Grove, Cook County, and Kane County, used two undercover agents, unspecified electronic surveillance, audio recordings, license-plate-reader data, and surveillance cameras from Chicago’s sprawling CCTV network to identify the April 2 event’s attendees and build connections to other anti-ICE protests in Illinois and other states.

Records produced by the Kane County Sheriff’s office show the unit in question is the Chicago-area Homeland Security Investigations Task Force. Local cops who join the unit are accepted on either a full- or part-time basis for two years, with the sign-off of their superiors.

Since Operation Midway Blitz in Illinois last autumn, residents in Chicago and surrounding communities have pressed elected officials for answers about cooperation between local law enforcement and ICE. While Illinois state law prevents local law enforcement from participating in immigration enforcement operations, criminal investigations by HSI appear to fall outside those restrictions because they pertain to ICE’s other law enforcement functions.

Chicago police have substantial approval requirements for surveilling and infiltrating protests or public gatherings, per a 2017 departmental order. It is unclear how these regulations apply to Chicago Police Department agents participating in joint task forces with federal agencies.

In response to questions from WIRED, the CPD claims that no First Amendment notifications to CPD’s chain of command or the department’s general counsel were made for the April 2 investigation, and says its work with HSI is permitted under Illinois law because it pertained to criminal enforcement, not immigration. A source with knowledge of the matter stated that CPD believed the early April surveillance operation was in service of a federal racketeering investigation. The only financial element mentioned in the report is a GoFundMe account to support the Crimethinc speaking tour. There is no mention of funds solicited or received at the April 2 event.

The Chicago Mayor’s Office did not provide comment ahead of publication. In response to queries from WIRED, ICE would neither confirm nor deny details of past or present investigations and said individual agents are not authorized to speak to the media.

HSI is also emerging as the point agency for Trump’s war on internal dissent and opposition politicians: Its agents, not the FBI, conducted the Minnesota 15 investigation and served a search warrant earlier this month on congresswoman Ilhan Omar of Minnesota.

The targeted April 2 event was held at Co-Prosperity, a Leftist gallery in the Southside Chicago neighborhood of Bridgeport. Several speakers from the Twin Cities area attended the event to share information about resisting federal immigration surges. This was part of a national tour last spring that was organized by the longtime anarchist collective Crimethinc, whose meetings have been infiltrated by federal informants repeatedly over the decades and contributed to the convictions of Eric McDavid, as well as 2008 Republican National Convention protester Matthew DePalma.

Per the four-page HSI report, a plainclothes cop from suburban Aurora was dropped off at the Co-Prosperity event by Officer Mike Xu from the wealthy village of Downers Grove; HSI undercover agent #9147 walked in five minutes later, once the undercover cop from Aurora confirmed no security checks were done at the door.

The undercover HSI agent transmitted a live recording of the event to the rest of the surveillance team for an hour, until transmission was lost. The recordings of the incident were uploaded and shared with HSI agent Kenneth Popovits, a financial crimes investigations supervisor who was working on a temporary assignment in Minneapolis.

Remarks by defendant Callum Robinet from the April 2 talk in Bridgeport about building rapid-response networks are included in the federal conspiracy indictment brought against the Minnesota 15, implicating the Chicagoland law enforcement that participated in surveillance in the controversial prosecution.

The Aurora Police Department, Downer’s Grove Police Department, and Kane County Sheriff did not respond to requests for comment.

The HSI Chicago surveillance team logged 219 vehicles parked in the vicinity of Co-Prosperity. Several subjects already under investigation, including some of the speakers on the tour, were identified by ICE. Ryan Wasson, a Kane County Sheriff’s deputy, spotted a hooded, masked man outside the venue removing an object from his trunk. Running the license plate, Wasson identified the man as the subject of an October 4 arrest during Operation Midway Blitz whose prosecution had been declined by assistant US attorney Christopher Veatch.

Outside the venue, agents Joel Sullivan and Michael Finerty spotted an unnamed subject already under investigation for running “an anti-ICE Signal chat and group called ‘ICE Cream Patrol’” and noted an orange Hyundai had been documented near two March 2026 protests outside the ICE detention center in Broadview, Illinois. The surveillance lasted for roughly two hours until 8:30 pm local time on April 2.

Nick Nunez, a veteran Chicago Police detective detailed to the joint task force, filed a request to obtain surveillance footage from a CPD camera outside the venue; the request would take a week to complete, and it is unclear whether he received footage. All information obtained during the April investigation in Chicago was shared with HSI St. Paul/Minnesota, according to the agency’s report.

Ed Yohnka, a spokesman for the American Civil Liberties Union of Illinois, calls the surveillance of the Bridgeport gathering “very troubling,” adding that local law enforcement should make a full accounting of their role in the incident and participation in joint task forces with federal agencies.

“No one should be subjected to surveillance and infiltration by law enforcement simply for engaging in constitutionally protected activity, including the ability to gather together and express dissatisfaction with public policy,” Yohnka says. “It is also a moment for local law enforcement to reexamine their participation in task forces with the Trump administration, especially any group focused on combatting ‘terrorism.’”

The April 2 meeting in Bridgeport is not the only out-of-state gathering spied upon by HSI during the Minnesota 15 investigation. Reports obtained by defense attorneys through discovery show that events on the same Crimethinc tour in Ann Arbor, Michigan, and Seattle, Washington, were monitored in person by local HSI agents, who reported the speech’s contents back to their colleagues in Minneapolis.
