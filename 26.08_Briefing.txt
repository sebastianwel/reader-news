[1] BEGINNT IN NAMIBIA DER GROSSFLÄCHIGE PHOSPHATABBAU IM MEER?

Vor der Küste Namibias könnte der weltweit erste große Abbau von Phosphat am Meeresboden beginnen. Die Genehmigungen werden derzeit erteilt. Das sorgt für massive Proteste von Küstenbewohnern - und selbst von der Präsidentin.

Es sind Hunderte Menschen, die mit Protestplakaten durch Namibias Hafenstadt Walvis Bay laufen. "Schützt unseren Ozean, schützt unsere Fischindustrie", steht auf den Plakaten, und: "Kein Fisch, keine Jobs, keine Zukunft".

Etwa 60 Kilometer vor der Küste will das Unternehmen Namibian Marine Phosphate im Atlantik Phosphat abbauen, in 200 bis 300 Meter Tiefe, auf einer Fläche fast so groß wie das Saarland. Staatliche Behörden erteilten jetzt die notwendigen Umweltgenehmigungen. Dann könnte dort der weltweit erste große Abbau von Phosphat am Meeresboden beginnen.

Der Aufschrei ist groß. Auch in der Politik ist das seit Jahren geplante Projekt "Sandpiper", zu deutsch "Strandläufer", höchst umstritten. Selbst Namibias Staatspräsidentin Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah und die Jugendorganisation der Regierungspartei SWAPO haben sich den Protesten angeschlossen.

Auch für Deutschland ist relevant, welchen politische Kurs Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah einschlägt.

Die Protestler von Walvis Bay haben eine Petition an die Regierung in Windhoek gerichtet. "Wir fordern die Regierung der Republik Namibia auf, alle Fragen im Zusammenhang mit Umweltgenehmigungen oder -zertifikaten für den marinen Phosphatabbau zu überdenken und zu prüfen, ob alle möglichen Auswirkungen auf Umwelt, Fischerei, Beschäftigung und die sozioökonomische Lage angemessen bewertet wurden", so ihre Forderung.

Phosphat wird weltweit gebraucht, vor allem für die Herstellung von Düngemittel. Auf dem Meeresboden vor der Küste Namibias entsteht der begehrte Stoff durch Bakterien, die abgestorbene Algen und andere Biomasse zersetzen.

Die Abbaupläne sehen vor, von einem Schiff aus mit einem ausklappbaren riesigen Saugrohr den Meeresboden etwa zweieinhalb Meter tief abzusaugen. Fünfeinhalb Millionen Tonnen Sediment könnten pro Jahr durch das Rohr an die Oberfläche gebracht werden. Was kein Phosphat enthält, würde vom Schiff direkt zurück ins Meer gehen.

Umweltschutzorganisationen und Fischereiverbände sehen darin eine große Gefahr, denn der Meeresboden enthält auch Schwermetalle und andere giftige Stoffe. Sie könnten freigesetzt werden und letztlich Namibias Fischbestände gefährden, sagen sie.

Eine kanadische Firma wittert riesige Ölvorkommen in Namibia.

Das Abbauunternehmen, das zum größten Teil einem Bergbaukonzern mit Sitz im Oman gehört, bestreitet das. Der Meeresboden werde weit weniger beeinträchtigt als durch die Schleppnetze der Fischer am Grund und den seit 30 Jahren praktizierten Abbau von Diamanten in der Tiefe.

Die Dozentin für Meeresforschung an der Universität von Namibia, Dietlinde Nakwaya-Jakobus, sagte dem öffentlich-rechtlichen namibischen Sender NBC, der Phosphatabbau könne sogar eine Chance sein, den Meeresboden mehr in den Blick zu nehmen.

"Dieser Ozean birgt zahlreiche Ressourcen. Ich habe das Gefühl, dass das Phosphat, was dort gefunden worden ist, uns als namibische Nation aufwecken kann. In der Genehmigung ist jedoch vermerkt, dass die Renaturierung dieser Gebiete überwacht werden wird", so Nakwaya-Jakobus. "Wir müssen die Überwachung und Kontrolle verstärken und sicherstellen, dass die Projektverantwortlichen das Richtige tun."

Das Abbauunternehmen Namibian Marine Phosphate, das seit 17 Jahren das Projekt vorantreibt, hat mitgeteilt, es werde noch dauern, bis tatsächlich ein Schiff mit Saugrohr auslaufen kann. Noch fehlten zahlreiche weitere Genehmigungen, unter anderem für eine Fabrik an Land, die den Phosphatsand verarbeitet.

Die Menschen in und um Walvis Bay aber wollen nicht warten, bis es so weit kommt. Der frühere Bürgermeister Trevino Forbes sagt: "Wir sind zusammengekommen - Bürgerinnen und Bürger, politische Parteien und verschiedene Gruppierungen -, um uns gegen das zu wehren, was gerade geschieht. Wir stehen in dieser Sache geschlossen zusammen und appellieren an die Verantwortlichen, dies nicht weiter zuzulassen."

Die Petition ist mittlerweile in der Hauptstadt Windhoek angekommen. Das Umweltministerium hat angekündigt, sie zu prüfen.

[1] FC BAYERN VERLÄNGERT MIT EBERL UND DREESEN BIS 2029

Der FC Bayern München setzt auf Kontinuität: Die Verträge mit Sportvorstand Max Eberl und dem Vorstandsvorsitzenden Jan-Christian Dreesen wurden jetzt bis 2029 verlängert. Das beschloss der Aufsichtsrat des Fußball-Rekordmeisters.

Nun also doch: Max Eberl darf den FC Bayern München als Sportvorstand in die Zukunft führen. Der Aufsichtsrat des Rekordmeisters bestätigte den 52-Jährigen auf seiner turnusmäßigen Sitzung und verlängerte dessen Vertrag bis 2029. Ebenfalls wurde dem Vorstandsvorsitzenden Jan-Christian Dreesen das Vertrauen ausgesprochen. Der Vertrag des 58-Jährigen wurde in der gleichen Sitzung ebenfalls bis 2029 verlängert.

In einer Vereinsmitteilung wird Herbert Hainer, der Vorsitzende des Aufsichtrates mit den Worten zitiert: "Mit Jan-Christian Dreesen, Max Eberl und Rouven Kasper ist unser Vorstand hervorragend aufgestellt, um den FC Bayern sportlich wie wirtschaftlich weiter voranzubringen und seine Spitzenposition in Europa zu behaupten."

Damit schließt der Rekordmeister seinen Frieden mit dem nicht immer unumstrittenen Sportvorstand. Für Max Eberl, der zuvor schon bei Borussia Mönchengladbach und RB Leipzig die sportlichen Geschicke als Sportdirektor bzw. Geschäftsführer Sport geleitet hatte, war sein Ex-Verein FC Bayern immer die Traumadresse. Doch bei den Münchnern gab es seit Eberls Amtsantritt am 1. März 2024 immer wieder mal Vorbehalte.

Noch im Mai, unmittelbar im Vorfeld des DFB-Pokalfinals in Berlin und dem Gewinn des Doubles, hatte Ehrenpräsident Uli Hoeneß Bedenken geäußert, ob Eberl der richtige Mann auf dieser Position sei. Seine Aussage "da sind noch Zweifel" und dass die Chancen auf eine Verlängerung bei 60:40 stünden, hatte seinerzeit für Irritationen gesorgt. Drei Monate später entschuldigte sich Hoeneß dann für seine Wortwahl und bezifferte die Wahrscheinlichkeit einer Vertragsverlängerung bereits auf 100 Prozent.

Dazwischen lag "eine perfekte Transferperiode" (Uli Hoeneß), in der Eberl mit ruhiger Hand und ohne viel Aufhebens Nägel mit Köpfen machte: Mit dem deutschen Nationalspieler Nathaniel Brown (Eintracht Frankfurt) und dem marokkanischen WM-Teilnehmer Ismael Saibari (PSV Eindhoven) lotste Eberl zwei Profis für jeweils rund 50 Millionen Euro Ablöse nach München, die den ohnehin schon starken Kader in der Breite noch verbessern dürften.

Auch einige talentierte Youngster wie Bara Sapoko Ndiaye band er an den Klub. Zuvor waren bereits die Transfers der Leistungsträger Michael Olise und Luis Diaz grandios eingeschlagen. Gleichzeitig konnte Eberl einige Verkäufe für sich verbuchen.

Der FC Bayern hat auch den auslaufenden Vertrag von Vorstandschef Jan-Christian Dreesen verlängert. Der 58-Jährige wird nun bis 2029 in herausragender Position bei den Münchnern bleiben. Wie bei Eberl hatte es zwischenzeitlich auch bei Dreesen Gerüchte um eine baldige Abberufung gegeben. Die haben sich nun als nicht stichhaltig herausgestellt.

Bis 2023 bestritt Nkunku 172 Pflichtspiele für RB, erzielte 70 Tore und lieferte 56 Vorlagen. In der Saison 2021/22 wurde er mit Leipzig DFB-Pokalsieger, ein Jahr später folgte die Titelverteidigung.

Nkunku lief zuletzt für die AC Mailand auf. (Archiv)

Nach seinem Abschied aus Leipzig war er zunächst zum FC Chelsea gewechselt. In der vergangenen Saison kam der 18-malige französische Nationalspieler dort auf 35 Pflichtspiele mit acht Toren und drei Vorlagen. Sein Vertrag bei den Rossoneri läuft eigentlich noch bis 2030.

Der Franzose kann im Zentrum hinter einer Spitze, als Flügelspieler und je nach System auch als beweglicher Stürmer eingesetzt werden. Seine Dribbelstärke, sein Tempo zwischen den Linien und seine Torgefahr sollen Leipzigs Offensive zusätzliche Unberechenbarkeit verleihen. [1] ‘DARTH VADER’ WANTS YOU TO KNOW HE DEFINITELY SUPPORTS FLOCK SURVEILLANCE

At the San Diego City Council’s most recent budget meeting, Sith Lord Darth Vader had some points to make. Between gasping breaths, Vader praised the council’s recent votes to renew its contract with the surveillance company Flock Safety, which has been making headlines recently as more and more stories of police officers abusing its tools have continued to come out—and as a WIRED review of code for a new AI tool already in use by some police departments has made clear that it is watching far more than just license plates.

“The emperor is a fan of Flock. And we must continue utilizing Flock technologies so that we can follow and surveil the rebel scum as they move from playground to playground, from playground to pool, from pool to gymnasium.”

The recording of the video has gone viral, reaching millions of people on TikTok and Instagram.

The man behind the stunt is Anthony Ralphs, a San Diego-based DJ, event producer, and agriculture consultant who has spent the better part of the past year advocating against the city’s support of Flock’s use by the San Diego Police Department.

Initially, Ralphs thought about keeping his identity hidden behind the Vader mask—a 2004 Hasbro model. But he says that as the video has picked up more and more steam, he decided to “take ownership” of the stunt that put Flock and surveillance technology on the minds and feeds of millions of people around the world.

WIRED spoke to Ralphs about advocacy, crafting a costume, and the role that art can play in civic engagement.

This conversation has been edited and condensed for clarity.

VITTORIA ELLIOTT: How did this start for you? Was this your first city council meeting?

ANTHONY RALPHS: No. My first time attending a city council meeting was sometime last summer or last fall. But really the most consequential city council meeting that I attended, that really got me in the fold of starting to regularly participate in my civic duties, was this issue of Flock. It was my third, or maybe my fourth, city council meeting back on December 9th, and the city council was putting this up to a vote, whether to continue to use Flock.

The story back then was that the city council didn't exactly know what the technology was. The police were touting it as a force multiplier. There were over 300 people at that vote, the meeting room was completely full, and I would say 95 percent of the people there were saying they didn’t want Flock. But the council voted for it anyway. It was extremely discouraging, and it really lit a fire under me to start going and regularly participating.

I came to the following city council meeting, and I was one of maybe five people in the city council chambers, and there had been close to 300 just a couple of weeks prior. That moment was eye-opening. I realized not everyone can be there all the time to do this, and that's what we need. We need consistent advocacy. So I took time off of school to be able to do that.

Our city's $120 million deficit. They cut several arts programs, and while they did that, they also provided the $2.2 million for Flock's contract, even when people in those communities that are being most heavily policed say that they don’t want this. And now, we have the case of Hugo Parra, a local San Diegan who was falsely imprisoned and sat in prison for 30 days due to an erroneous tag by the Flock cameras. [Flock did not immediately respond to a request for comment on this matter.]

He and his friend, who was also arrested, are both suing this city for $1.5 million. So by the time the city ends up settling those two lawsuits, it will probably add another million dollars to that contract.

I was taking classes at San Diego Mesa College and San Diego City College, I had been studying theater and labor studies, and I got my certificate in labor studies. As a student, I interned for the American Federation of Teachers, the local chapter here in San Diego. Our job was to organize and we would get real hands-on training going to strike lines and showing solidarity. We would canvas for candidates, and we were doing phone banks, so we got a really comprehensive experience of what it's like to participate civically in the real world. I would DJ for the people on the picket line once a week before school, and that really showed me the power of art specifically to make a political impact.

What ultimately led to the choice to attend the council meeting as Darth Vader?

I was always interested in guerrilla theater, I studied it in a playwriting class in school. But this was really a last resort. I had been going to meetings for months, and I have a lot of sympathy for these representatives, because I am the vice chair of my local planning group. I know what it’s like to sit there and get dunked on by random people. So, I did my homework on all of them. I learned what they voted for, what they've voted against, the progressive policies that they had enacted that I could support. I went so far as to bring them flowers.

But at a certain point, it felt like we've exhausted all of the efforts. We did it the right way, and they still continued to ignore their constituents.

I was in the shower one day and it hit me. I was trying to think of how I could satirize their points, because I realized they seemed to have a lack of self-awareness. I realized we're not getting through to them, and they are not listening to us and there's no consequences for them not listening to us. So, maybe we need to show them how silly they're being.

I am a huge Star Wars fan. I used to watch the movies almost every day as a kid. In the lead-up to the meeting, I rewatched all the movies again, the original ones, trying to get his gesticulations down. I got a silk cape, I think it was from Turkey. I found the original 2004 Hasbro helmet, and I chose this one because it had a voice changer in it. But I had been told the voice changer was a little bit gimmicky, so I thought “I'll just replicate his voice myself.” I wasn’t able to get everything I wanted for the costume, but I knew that the August 19th meeting was going to be the big one, because the ACLU was going to be at that committee meeting. So I pulled together some stuff I already had, and I think it turned out well.

The helmet actually came in handy, because there were buttons on it to make noises. At certain points, I'd stand up to go to the bathroom and I'd hit the breathing button—you know, the Darth Vader breathing sound. At one point I turned around, and I'm right within earshot of the chief of police, and I pushed the button and it said, “Your powers are weak!”

I want to make it cool to engage in one's civic responsibility again. City council meetings don't have to be stale rooms with boring thoughts presented in unoriginal ways. They can be places where we bring our own art, where we bring the strength of our intellect and our moral fortitude with our talent and creativity.

This sparked a global conversation about luxury surveillance and living under Big Brother, and in Orwellian-digital, fully surveilled hellscape. Some people didn't know what Flock was a week ago. We've reframed the narrative from “this is a ‘necessary evil’” to “if Darth Vader finds it a ‘necessary evil,’ that should make us pause and reflect on whether or not we need it.”

[3] GOOGLE PIXEL 11 PRO FOLD REVIEW: BETTER, NOT BEST

The Google Pixel 11 Pro Fold is a predictably iterative improvement over last year’s foldable, with slight upgrades to performance and the camera, but it comes with an unwelcome $100 price bump. The IP68 rating for water and dust resistance and the Qi2 25W support for faster magnetic charging are standouts in a category where Google is lagging, but it has never been known for its hardware. Software is where the company excels, and some folks will appreciate the unrivaled AI-infused software features.

Google is good at elevating its devices beyond the spec sheet, but the 11 Pro Fold is perilously close to the Pixel 11 on paper, a phone that’s less than half its price. Beyond the big unfolding screen and customizable rear LEDs, little else justifies the “Pro” tag. With Samsung’s fresh Fold8 form factor and a folding iPhone on the horizon, I’m not convinced Google has done enough to make this the foldable you drop $1,900 on.

The Pixel 11 Pro Fold doesn’t look different from the 10 Pro Fold, but Google has shaved off a fraction of a millimeter and 19 grams. Sadly, the 11 Pro Fold is still heavier and thicker (239 grams and 10.1 mm) than Samsung’s Galaxy Z Fold8 (201 grams and 9.7 mm), the Fold8 Ultra (215 grams and 8.9 mm), and the Honor Magic V6 (219 grams and 8.75 mm, not sold in the US). That makes it a little trickier to handle, though the horizontal camera bar means less rocking than Samsung’s phones when they lie flat.

The double-camera module has had a makeover, so it looks less chunky, and the back is matte-textured glass that feels lovely. The Pixel 11 Pro Fold scores an impressive IP68 rating for water and dust resistance. That compares favorably to Samsung’s IP48 ratings, which means both can survive shallow submersion, but Google’s phone is dust-tight, whereas Samsung’s phones are at risk from fine dust and sand.

It’s a shame the Fold is only available in two colors: Obsidian, the color of my review unit, looks more like a dark matte gray than black, while Olive Green has a shinier gold frame.

I’m not a fan of Google’s asymmetrical design. With the Pixel 11 Pro Fold closed, there’s a curved corner cutout, and when you open the phone, the two halves have slightly different thicknesses. All folding phones still have a detectable fold line in the middle of the screen when you open them, but Google’s is among the most noticeable. Some folks won’t notice or care, but I’m the kind of guy who goes upstairs to turn the light on so the downstairs switches aren’t misaligned.

The fingerprint sensor in the power button always bugs me when I switch to a folding phone, because I have to pick it up to touch it and unlock the screen, rather than pressing on the screen like most phones. You do have face unlock with the Pixel 11 Pro Fold, and it works well provided there’s ample light, but it still doesn’t work in a dark room, which is still annoying.

You get two lovely, sharp OLED displays in the Pixel 11 Pro Fold: an 8-inch main screen and a 6.5-inch cover screen. Both push peak brightness to 3,600 nits, remaining legible in direct sunlight, and boast a variable refresh rate up to 120 Hz. They’re smooth, with rich colors, and I have nothing to complain about beyond that crease.

I’m not convinced the 11 Pro Fold earns the “Pro” in its title. It does have the new HiLight feature, a group of color LEDs in the camera module’s flash that light up different colors based on who is calling, or swirl through the rainbow while you chat to Gemini. It’s neat, if a little underbaked. It’s handy if you put your phone face down when you’re working or trying to relax, but it’s a very minor feature.

All the Pixels get the new Tensor G6 processor, and the Fold has 16 GB of RAM paired with 256 GB, 512 GB, or 1 TB of storage. Switching from the Pixel 10 Pro XL, it was mostly smooth sailing. But it froze a couple of times in split-screen view in Gmail and after launching an app on the outer display, then unfolding the inner display.

The 11 Pro Fold actually has more in common with the regular Pixel 11, because it misses out on the high-end camera in its Pro siblings. Folding phones struggle to match flagships on camera specs because of form-factor limitations, and they must include two front-facing cameras (one for open and one for closed).

You get the same 48-megapixel main camera and 10.8-megapixel telephoto for 5X optical zoom as on the Pixel 11, but the Fold drops to a 10.5-megapixel ultrawide. The main camera is excellent in a wide range of scenarios, and I used the telephoto lens a lot. The 5X zoom produces sharp, detailed shots, though things get a little fuzzy if you push up to the maximum digital zoom capability at 30X. The ultrawide is a very obvious weak spot, producing relatively grainy images, though it doubles as a macro lens for decent, if slightly distorted, close-ups. The 10-megapixel front-facing cameras are passable for selfies.

The 11 Pro and 11 Pro XL cameras are superior across the board (50-MP main, 48-MP ultrawide, 48-MP telephoto, and 42-MP front-facing). They also boast instant Night Sight, 120X digital zoom, and better video capture. That said, I took some great shots with the Pixel 11 Pro Fold; its camera results compare favorably to other foldables, and a good telephoto is more useful than an ultrawide.

I like the new camera tricks: Camera Looks lets you define your preferred style, dictating contrast and colors; Magic Capture makes it easy to get video and photos from an event without focusing on the viewfinder; and Creator Suite will delight content creators with some useful tools, like a built-in teleprompter. You can even use Circle to Search in the camera app to get info on anything you circle without snapping a shot first.

Unfortunately, there’s one significant area where the Fold looks amateur, and that’s battery life. The 4,806-mAh battery is smaller than the one in last year’s 10 Pro Fold (5,015 mAh). While that downgrade is mitigated by greater power efficiency in the new Tensor chip, Google suggests 24 hours for the 11 Pro Fold, compared to 30-plus for the other Pixel 11 models.

Compared to a folding phone with a silicon-carbon battery, like the Honor Magic V6, the Pixel 11 Pro Fold lacks stamina, but it shouldn’t be an issue for most folks. The battery lost 6 percent in an hour of video streaming, and an hour of casual gaming claimed 9 percent. It will get you through an average day easily, but when I pushed the Fold with a lot of photography and graphically demanding gaming, I had to engage the battery saver before bedtime.

There’s 30-watt wired charging, but you should definitely embrace Qi2 wireless charging with the Pixel 11 Pro Fold as it tops out at 25 watts and is so much more convenient. The magnets stick firmly and ensure perfect alignment. It’s nice to see Qi2 25W in a folding phone, since many competitors haven’t even adopted it in their regular flagships yet.

I’m a big fan of Google’s Pixel-exclusive software features. Call Screen saves me from telemarketers daily; I use Now Playing often to add songs to my playlist, and several new features may just persuade you to board the Pixel train. My colleague Julian Chokkattu talked about Rambler in his Pixel 11 Pro review as a more conversational voice-to-text message-drafting tool. I also like that AI overviews pop up contextually to save you a step, offering a Calendar link when someone texts to suggest a meet-up, for example, though it does mean granting Gemini access.

But one of the most useful new features for the Fold is Bubbles. With this, you can keep a floating circular profile picture from an open chat and open or minimize it with a tap. It’s not a new idea (Facebook Chat Heads did it more than a decade ago), but it lets you get on with other things without leaving the chat, potentially floating above two apps in split-screen mode. It also minimizes to a bar at the bottom right when you’re using the main screen.

With folding phones, people always talk about the ability to watch movies and play games on a big screen, but their real strength is multitasking. Message apps can show the list and a preview simultaneously. You can read a café review and check the location on a map, or watch a movie while you skim your emails.

That said, many apps still aren’t optimized for folding phones, so they can look clownishly big or occasionally obscure buttons. As mentioned, I ran into freezing issues and had to restart apps more than once. Sometimes, when you switch from the cover display to the big screen, it fails to scale up, and you might get a prompt to restart the game for full-screen view, as I did with Bloons TD 6.

Foldable newcomers should also beware of the big black bars. You must rotate the opened Fold for maximum screen viewing, but virtually nothing is made for this weird, almost square aspect ratio. You get more room when browsing something like Netflix, but start a video and you’re not much better off than with a large flagship phone like the Pixel 11 Pro XL.

Games fare a little better, and the 11 Pro Fold is great for titles like Clash of Clans, point-and-click adventures like Machinarium, and tower defense games like Bloons TD 6. It’s also a good option for older games via emulators, but for anything too demanding, like Wuthering Waves or Genshin Impact, you can expect dropped frames galore.

The other reason I find myself unfurling the big screen is to read. For browsing the web, doomscrolling Reddit, or, best of all, reading an ebook, a folding phone is a big step up from a regular phone screen. Whether it’s worth the premium you pay is debatable. It’s a little too easy to forget to use the big screen in daily life.

Folding phones have been around for years, but they still feel a bit gimmicky. For folks who think they’re a tough sell when they can get both a higher-spec flagship phone and a tablet for the price, it’s worth laying hands on one to get a feel for the experience. Foldables may not be an inevitable next step for phones, but there’s a reason they’ve carved out a niche.

The 11 Pro Fold starts at $1,899; that’s $100 more than last year’s 10 Pro Fold, but it doesn’t have a storage bump to soften the blow, as the Google Pixel 11 ($899) does. It also lacks the Pro features you’ll find in the Pixel 11 Pro and Pro XL ($1,099 and $1,299). If you’re set on a foldable, the Samsung Galaxy Z Fold8 and Fold8 Ultra ($1,900 and $2,100) demand consideration, and if you don’t live in the US, check out the Honor Magic V6 ($2,100). There are other options and form factors in our best folding phones, but rumors of a folding iPhone persist, so you might want to wait and see what that looks like.
