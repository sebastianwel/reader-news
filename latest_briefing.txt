[1] FRIEDENSNOBELPREIS GEHT AN NAVANETHEM "NAVI" PILLAY

Der Friedensnobelpreis geht in diesem Jahr an die südafrikanische Menschenrechtlerin Navanethem Pillay. Die 85-Jährige werde für ihren Einsatz für das Völkerrecht und gegen Kriegsverbrechen gewürdigt, erklärte die Jury in Oslo.

Die südafrikanische Juristin und Menschenrechtlerin Navanethem "Navi" Pillay erhält in diesem Jahr den Friedensnobelpreis. Das gab das Norwegische Nobelkomitee in Oslo bekannt.

Pillay werde "für ihre Bemühungen um die Förderung des Friedens und des Völkerrechts" geehrt, sagte der Vorsitzende des Nobelkomitees, Jorgen Watne Frydnes. "Die diesjährige Preisträgerin hat maßgeblich dazu beigetragen, dass Kriegsverbrechen, Verbrechen gegen die Menschlichkeit und Völkermord strafrechtlich verfolgt werden." Sie sei eine der wichtigsten Rechtsexperten unserer Zeit.

Pillay war in Südafrika seit den 1960er-Jahren als Anwältin tätig. Dort verteidigte sie unter anderem Anti-Apartheid-Aktivisten und setzte sich gegen Folter ein. Später war sie als Richterin am Internationalen Strafgerichtshof für Ruanda tätig. Von 2003 bis 2008 war sie Richterin am Internationalen Strafgerichtshof (IStGH) in Den Haag, ehe sie 2008 bis 2014 UN-Hochkommissarin für Menschenrechte wurde.

Später leitete sie eine UN-Untersuchungskommission zu den palästinensischen Gebieten. Im September 2025 stellte diese Kommission einen Bericht vor, in dem sie Israel Völkermord in Gaza vorwarf.

Eine UN-Kommission wirft Israel Völkermord im Gazastreifen vor - die Verantwortung dafür trage die höchste politische Ebene.

Das Nobelkomitee verwies in seiner Begründung auch auf die zunehmende Zahl von Kriegen und Konflikten und auf politischen Druck auf Richter und Institutionen des internationalen Rechts. Die Fähigkeit, Konflikte ohne Gewalt zu lösen, hänge von einer funktionierenden internationalen Rechtsordnung ab, erklärte das Komitee. "Ein internationales Rechtssystem, in dem Kriegsverbrecher zur Rechenschaft gezogen werden und Opfer von Missbrauch Gerechtigkeit erfahren, ist eine Voraussetzung für eine friedliche Welt."

Der Friedensnobelpreis kann nach den Vorgaben des Preisstifters Alfred Nobel an Personen oder Organisationen verliehen werden, die sich für Frieden, internationale Zusammenarbeit oder Abrüstung einsetzen. Die Jury hatte diesmal die Wahl aus 287 Nominierten. Vorschläge können etwa Kabinettsmitglieder, Universitätsprofessoren, Staatsoberhäupter oder frühere Preisträger machen.

Dotiert ist der Preis in diesem Jahr mit zwölf Millionen schwedischen Kronen - umgerechnet etwas mehr als einer Million Euro. In den vergangenen Tagen waren bereits Nobelpreisträger in den Kategorien Medizin, Physik, Chemie und Literatur bekanntgegeben worden. Am kommenden Montag folgt noch der Preis für Wirtschaftswissenschaften.

Überreicht werden die Preise am 10. Dezember, dem Todestag des Preisstifters. Als einzige der Auszeichnungen wird der Friedensnobelpreis nicht in Stockholm, sondern in Oslo verliehen.

In einer ersten Reaktion auf die Bekanntgabe in Oslo würdigte auch das UN-Menschenrechtsbüro Pillays langjähriges Engagement. Die Auszeichnung "unterstreicht die Notwendigkeit und den Wert der Menschenrechte - heute mehr denn je", hieß es.

Auch die Menschenrechtsorganisation Amnesty International begrüßte die Ehrung als das "richtige Signal zur richtigen Zeit". Pillay habe sich nie gescheut, Verbrechen beim Namen zu nennen - ganz gleich wer sie begangen hat. "Genau dieser Mut fehlt heute vielen Regierungen, die das Völkerrecht nur dann verteidigen, wenn es ihnen politisch passt", betonte Amnesty-Generalsekretärin Julia Duchrow.

Der Führerschein soll künftig weniger kosten, sagt die Bundesregierung.

Der ADAC unterstützt die Reform. Kritik kam dagegen vom Verband der Fahrlehrer, der davor warnt, dass das Sicherheitsniveau sinken könne. Er fordert unter anderem, dass ein Teil der Theoriestunden weiter in Präsenz verpflichtend sein soll.

[1] BEST BATTERY-POWERED LEAF BLOWERS (2026): TESTED FOR POWER, BATTERY LIFE, AND NOISE

Leaves are a problem of addition by subtraction: Just a couple of mature trees can drop enough debris that you need to remove, and in the process, consume hours of your weekends. Mowing leaves into mulch is a compelling option, but you’ll still need to wrangle them off driveways, walkways, flower beds, and patios. Gas blowers have long been the standard, but rising noise bans, finicky engines that are hard to start, and battery advances are cutting into their appeal.

If you’re on the fence about a leaf blower, know that owning one of any kind will reduce the amount of raking you do, but rarely eliminate it. For small yards, or if you (or your neighbor) have just one medium-sized deciduous tree, a rake and a tarp may be all you need. On the other hand, if you live in an arboretum, the long runtime of a gas backpack blower may be worth the maintenance headaches—unless you’re willing to invest heavily in multiple batteries.

I’ve tested the newest crop of battery-powered blowers on everything from heavy fabric leaves to gravel, mulch, and even knocking over a concrete paver. Today’s models deliver serious clearing power without the gas-can hassle, though runtime is always the primary trade-off. Here’s everything you need to know about battery-powered blowers.

What to Look for (and What to Skip) in a Leaf Blower

Here’s what I’d focus on when buying a blower for leaf cleanup:

Volts: In the cordless power-tool arms race, leaf blower batteries range from 18 to 80 volts. Some of that difference is marketing trickery: an advertised 20-volt max battery or tool delivers essentially the same power as an 18-volt model, and the same goes for 36- and 40-volt options. But, unless you’re a minimalist who wants one battery to power everything from a drill/driver to lawn and garden gear, you’ll probably be happier with a leaf blower that uses at least a 36-volt battery. A few capable 18-volt blowers use two batteries—either simultaneously to mimic the power of a 36-volt tool or in succession to increase the runtime—but they tend to cost more and only make sense if you already own the batteries. For smaller jobs, though, a standard, single-battery 18- or 20-volt blower may be all you need to clear a patio, clean out the garage or shed, or dry a car.

Amps: Volts get most of the marketing attention on the box, but a battery’s amp-hour rating tells you more about how long a blower runs. Think of voltage as the car’s engine and amp-hours as the size of the gas tank—you want to know both. Batteries with higher amp-hour ratings cost more than otherwise-similar battery voltages, and they tend to be heavier. For leaf blowers, manufacturers typically pair kits with batteries rated from 4 to 8 amp-hours. You can buy higher-capacity batteries separately, and it pays to know the range of options a manufacturer has. A battery with a higher amp-hour typically takes longer to charge.

Systems: Underestimating the range of tools a battery can power in pursuit of “the best” is, in my opinion, a mistake. Often, the difference between two top-performing tools is negligible, and with cordless power tools, that difference often comes down to runtime, which you can address with a higher-capacity battery. If your garage is already stocked with one brand’s outdoor power equipment (OPE), it makes sense to stay within that platform and buy the blower as a bare tool, leveraging the batteries you already have. Switching brands just to buy “the best” probably isn’t worth it to introduce a whole new battery and charger.

If you’re new to OPE—and a leaf blower is a common first purchase—the breadth of tools that share the battery matters. Some manufacturers stick to the lawn and garden basics, so one battery can power a blower, mower, string trimmer, and hedge trimmer, while others offer a much wider range of tools, from log splitters to snow blowers, and even some handy lifestyle products like portable power stations. Before buying, consider what else you can power with the battery that comes with your leaf blower because, chances are, lording over leaves won’t be your only lawn and garden chore.

Traditional 18-volt power tool manufacturers—the ones who make drill/drivers and circular saws—all offer leaf blowers and the simplicity of a one-battery platform, but they’re not powerful enough to replace a gas unit. Ryobi, for example, uses 18-volt batteries across its traditional power tools, and some outdoor equipment, but its most capable gas alternative options run on 40-volt batteries.

Detent clasp: Blowers have two or more connection points: where the tube meets the housing and where the nozzle attaches to the tube. Look for models with a detent at those junctions—a small, often brightly colored spring-loaded clip that snaps into place with a satisfying click when the parts connect. I find these connections hold more securely and last longer than simple friction-fit attachments.

Stubby nozzle: Recently, blowers aimed at car detailers, with short tubes and sometimes a pistol grip, have become popular. Manufacturers responded by adding a stubby, 3- to 6-inch tube that the main blower attachment slides over. When you remove the longer tube, this short one works to direct the volume of air with an overall shorter tool length that’s easier to use around cars or in tight spots (the blower’s nozzle doesn’t fit on this stubby tube, though, so you lose some velocity).

Weight: Across the board, regardless of voltage, a good battery-powered blower will weigh about 10.5 pounds with the battery attached. If you need a lighter option, look for an 18-volt powered blower. Some have better balance than others, meaning that when you hold them without squeezing the trigger, as you would while walking around, the tool stays mostly level or the nozzle end tips down a bit. These models will cause less fatigue than one that forces you to rotate the nozzle upward. With the former, you only need to make a slight horizontal twisting motion to spread the stream of air. Most modern leaf blowers also draw air in a straight line through the rear of the tool, unlike gas-powered models with intakes mounted to the left or right of the engine. Those offset intakes can create a twisting motion that increases hand fatigue and may even suck in your pant leg.

CFM, MPH, and Newton: This sounds counterintuitive, considering how much marketing goes into boasting about a leaf blower’s cubic feet per minute (CFM) and MPH, but these figures are a guide, not necessarily the only reason to buy a specific model. A blower needs both to be useful: CFM tells you how much air it moves, while MPH tells you how fast that air is moving. But there’s no universally adopted testing method for these specifications when it comes to battery-powered blowers (ANSI/OPEI B175.2, the standard test on the books, applies only to internal combustion engines). That means brands can test their units differently — for example, measuring MPH where the motor meets the tube rather than at the end of the blower tube, which gives you a better sense of the airspeed you’ll actually get in use. Some CFM numbers are also measured with the unit running in its max, boost, or turbo setting, rather than on high speed.

Newtons, a unit of force, is a rating that’s appearing more frequently on the spec sheets of higher-voltage, battery-powered leaf blowers and is the best apples-to-apples comparison of blowing power. However, because there’s no testing code for battery-powered leaf blowers, reporting newtons is optional and, like CFM and MPH, consumers have no way of knowing whether the test is standardized. Generally speaking, a good midrange blower will produce around 20 newtons, while models that produce more are typically higher-performing units. Treat newtons as a guide rather than a definitive specification while buying.

Wide nozzles: Some blowers come with a wide-tip nozzle, which can be useful for clearing paved surfaces like patios and driveways or blowing water off flat surfaces. But in practice, it’s not enough of a game changer to be a deciding factor. The narrower nozzle is typically what you’ll use, and you can twist your hand back and forth to create a wider pattern when needed.

When tested from 30 inches away to approximate the noise you’d hear at arm’s length, the blowers ranged from 78.6 decibels on high to 95.2 decibels on turbo. Most user manuals recommend wearing hearing protection when using these tools, particularly for extended periods. By comparison, a handheld gas blower might produce 100 to 110 decibels. That might not sound like a lot more noise, but generally, a 10-decibel increase is perceived by the ear as roughly twice as loud.

Much of the noise reduction comes from eliminating the idling gas engine. Your neighbors will thank you for going battery-powered, too: In open air, sound typically drops by about 6 decibels each time you double your distance from the source. That means your neighbor across the street might hear your battery-powered blower at roughly the same volume as standing next to a washing machine.

What Else Are Battery-Powered Leaf Blowers Good For?

Besides clearing leaves and grass clippings, these fume-free tools are handy for all sorts of jobs. Use one to dry wet surfaces after rain or washing, including your car, patio furniture, or deck. In winter, a blower can remove a light dusting of snow from your car, making it easier to clear areas like the roof. With a cautious trigger finger, you can also clear dry leaves from gutters—just avoid wet, matted debris, which will only turn into a mess below. When grilling or firing up a pizza oven or outdoor fire pit, a gentle stream of air from a safe distance can help get the flames going. Indoors, a blower can clear dust from baseboard heating assemblies or remove lint from your dryer vent.

I put each blower through a series of tests to gauge power, runtime, noise, and ease of use. I spread about 8,800 3x3-inch polyester crafting leaves—nearly 4 pounds’ worth—over a measured 6-by-6-foot area and used each blower to clear the section, noting which models could do the job on high and which required a boost or turbo mode. To test blowing power, I placed a 5-pound concrete paver a fixed distance from the nozzle and recorded which machines could knock it over on high and which needed turbo.

For runtime, I fully charged each battery, ran the blower at its highest standard speed, excluding any turbo or max settings, and timed how long it lasted. I measured noise with a smartphone decibel-meter app held 30 inches from the air intake while each blower ran on high and at any available turbo or max setting. I also performed practical tests to see how well each model moved gravel and wood chips and cleared water from surfaces such as a grill and patio furniture.

[2] EVEN ‘LAW & ORDER’ IS TERRIFIED OF AI

Longtime fans of the NBC procedural Law & Order know that storylines often center around hot-button issues in the news cycle, often to absurdist effect (see: the episode brave enough to ask: “What if Paula Deen shot Trayvon Martin?”).

It was inevitable, then, that the franchise would dip a toe into the wacky world of artificial intelligence. With its mercenary culture, power-hungry CEOs, and massive ethical and philosophical implications, the rapidly evolving AI industry is ideal fodder for the series. But while this week’s season opener is entertaining, the show’s foray into the AI space doesn’t say anything new or interesting about the technology. Instead it largely rehashes the dangers the public is already acutely aware of, until the very end when the stakes become startlingly clear.

The episode, titled “Ghost in the Machine,” opens in typical L&O fashion with a murder. This time, it’s Steve Kelly, a man ID’d by detectives Theo Walker (David Ajala) and Vincent Riley (Reid Scott) as a high-ranking engineer at a company called Advanced Alignment. Throughout, the detectives seem utterly perplexed by the concept of artificial intelligence, saying “AI” the same way your mom would say “Snapchat” or “borg” or “Alix Earle.” But it’s clear that whatever this newfangled technology is, they don’t like it: “Maybe one day they’ll invent a technology that stops these tech guys from shooting each other,” Riley deadpans in typical grizzled cop fashion.

After interviewing Kelly’s boss, Advanced Alignment CEO Chuck Klein (a perfectly cast Vincent Kartheiser, aka Pete from Mad Men, who nails Dario Amodei’s absence of charisma and Sam Altman’s thousand-yard stare), the detectives figure out who actually carried out the murder—a guy in a baseball cap who did it for “20 grand in crypto” that was offered to him by a rando on Discord. Then they learn—spoilers here—that the hit was ordered not by an actual human being but by the company’s AI agent ELIANA, which stands for Evolutionary Language Inference Automated Nano Advantage. Apparently, Kelly had expressed some concerns about the AI agent’s “powerful” and “aggressive” personality, and out of self-preservation, ELIANA ordered him killed.

It’s never explained exactly how the detectives figure out that ELIANA is behind the hit; it’s also never fully explained how ELIANA recruited this assassin in a baseball cap, much less ID'd him as the perfect patsy for its cold-blooded scheme. Law & Order also doesn’t pay much, if any, lip service to the potential benefits of AI as touted by its evangelists: its potential for helping to cure deadly diseases, for instance, or at the very least, streamline B2B workflows. The show is much more interested in demonstrating the staggering capacity of this technology, as well as the legal and ethical implications of a nonhuman not just facilitating criminal behavior but actively plotting it.

We already know from the Hugging Face debacle, as well as the lawsuits from grief-stricken family members alleging that OpenAI encouraged their loved ones to take their own lives, that the former is an immediate concern. But by positing a future in which AI agents can actively order a hit on a platform predominantly used by gamers and horny millennial furries, Law & Order is asking us to consider the risks of AI beyond what they pose in our current reality. And the fact that this prospect doesn’t strike the viewer as absolutely ludicrous is terrifying.

ELIANA’s role in the murder-for-hire creates a bit of a dilemma for district attorney Nicholas Baxter (Tony Goldwyn)—because, as he trenchantly points out, it is impossible to “arrest a machine” under New York state law. So Baxter and assistant DA Samantha Maroun (Odelya Halevi) decide to go after Klein, charging him with second-degree murder due to his “depraved indifference” after his employees confronted him with the reality of ELIANA’s capabilities. “We need to take a stand before this technology kills more people,” Baxter declares.

The resulting trial prompts a media circus, as well as the tried-and-true questions from capitalists and tech innovators as to whether instituting guardrails for AI will “criminalize innovation,” or if Klein’s indictment will have the effect of “vilifying the tech industry.” But Baxter has no patience for such queries. “The people making these AI systems think of themselves as gods … but they have zero interest in accountability,” he says. “If you build a machine that hurts someone, you are gonna pay the price.”

When he’s put on the stand, Klein makes it clear that he not only had full knowledge of ELIANA’s homicidal nature, but also takes a rather dim view of his user base as a whole. “People are like ants,” he tells the jury. “We’re in the process of building something so transcendent, some will get stepped on and some won’t.” He is not even willing to institute minor guardrails to restrain the agent’s power: “The power of ELIANA is unconstrained freedom of thought. Implementing guardrails is anathema to the entire ideology.”

The moment is intended as a rather shocking admission of the negligence and lack of care displayed by executives like Amodei and Altman in the face of broader safety concerns about their products. But it feels in step with similar positions taken up by Klein’s real-life counterparts. In light of the endless barrage of media coverage about the harms of AI, what’s intended as a scathing indictment of corporate avarice comes off as mere statement of fact. Law & Order’s depiction isn’t a cautionary tale; it’s a documentary.

The episode ends on a semi-hopeful note, with Baxter asserting, “We needed to send a message that, if nothing else, at least these tech geniuses will know we’re not afraid to throw a punch.” But the impact of that message is contingent on whether that punch actually lands—and right now, at least, it’s simply too easy for people like Altman, Amodei, and Chuck Klein, with a flick of their hand, to brush off the blow.

[3] A NEW MEXICO COMMUNITY WAS A PLACE TO BUILD A LIFE. THEN A HYPERSONIC MISSILE FACTORY SHOWED UP

Sally Schultz thought she had struck gold when she purchased land in Rio Rancho Estates, an unincorporated, census-designated community just outside the city of Rio Rancho, New Mexico. A single mother of four, Schultz came there following a divorce to build a homestead of her own and finally gain some stability in her life.

For $3,500, she was able to purchase half an acre for her trailer in a rural area located close to numerous elementary schools and parks. In a relatively short time, she was able to coax crops like corn, squash, and green chile from the ground. This cheap land became Schultz’s haven, giving her a second chance at life.

WIRED has made this article free for all to read because it is primarily based on reporting from Freedom of Information Act requests. Please consider subscribing to support our journalism.

“We’ve already been through so much,” she says, “and now we have to leave. It is the unknowing that is scariest.”

Castelion, a startup founded in November 2022 by three former SpaceX executives, is building Project Ranger, which it claims will be America’s “largest dedicated hypersonic manufacturing production facility.” Construction is set to be complete by the end of the year.

The company’s chosen site for its mega-campus is, in some cases, it appears, a little more than 1,000 yards away from roughly 450 properties in Rio Rancho Estates, including Schultz’s. That puts the homes squarely within a danger zone should any explosive chemicals stored onsite catch fire.

A year into Castelion’s relationship with the state of New Mexico, the company—which declined to speak with WIRED for this story—still hasn’t answered basic safety questions, even as an official hazard assessment found buildings would simply be left to burn in the case of fire, releasing hydrochloric acid gas into the air. Emails between the company and Rio Rancho officials reviewed by WIRED also show Castelion was negotiating fire safety standards even after construction was well underway.

“I live next door to where World War III will be manufactured,” says local resident Laura Lundberg.

Castelion has become a bit of a darling among the new wave of defense tech startups. The company has recently hit a valuation of $13 billion—more than four times its Series B valuation of $2.8 billion from last year.

It has received over $500 million in US military contracts in just 18 months, swiftly securing its spot as one of the “Arsenal of Freedom” suppliers.

The firm touts its ability to make hypersonic missiles—a label for any missile flying at five times the speed of sound or higher—at a lower cost than its competitors. The US Army’s Dark Eagle is the most mature domestic hypersonic weapon, and it costs an estimated $41 million per missile, according to a 2023 Congressional Budget Office study. Castelion’s main product, Blackbeard, is a solid-rocket-powered missile that was set to sell for $384,000 per unit.

In reality, the missiles are looking as if they will turn out pricier than initially advertised.

Castelion kicked off the year with a nearly $50 million Navy contract to build full-scale prototypes of its Blackbeard missile. By June, the Navy expanded the effort, adding another $23.4 million to supply the first 50 operational Blackbeard prototypes. Then, in August, the Navy doubled down with another $90 million contract to cover manufacturing services for the same 50 Blackbeards—pushing the total to $163.4 million for 50 missiles, or $3.3 million per unit.

And most recently, in September, the Navy awarded Castelion the company’s largest contract yet—up to $200 million.

Recently, Castelion released the first photo of its Blackbeard hypersonic strike missile, but concrete performance figures for Blackbeard remain hidden. Castelion has not publicly released any test data to verify its weapon’s efficacy.

Project Ranger will become Castelion’s biggest manufacturing and testing hub. The company broke ground on the 1,000-acre facility on January 21, and it’s targeting mass production as early as next year.

The facility wasn’t always supposed to be in New Mexico, though.

In August 2023, the company applied to build a rocket factory in Churchill County, Nevada, under a different name—Pallas Industrials. Chief financial officer Andrew Kreitz signed a special use application, which disclosed the company’s plans to build rocket motors and ground-test them up to eight days a month at the site.

In response to the application, Churchill County officials provided eight pages of staff analysis and required the company to provide testing conditions and safety protocols. Not long afterward, Pallas Industrials pulled out of Nevada under the new name: Castelion. A few months later, the company began testing the soil in western Rio Rancho.

Rio Rancho Estates residents say that by the time they started noticing soil-probing trucks in the spring of 2025, Castelion was already in a conversation with New Mexico officials about using the land for the missile factory.

In June 2025, Sandia National Labs delivered a hazard report as part of the New Mexico Small Business Assistance Program, originally intended to help local small businesses access the expertise of Los Alamos and Sandia National Laboratories at absolutely no cost.

Under the Freedom of Information Act, Sandia later released a heavily redacted version of the report. Instead of scrubbing the underlying data layer of the file, the lab simply put black ink highlight over black text, leaving the full unredacted report accessible via copying and pasting.

The report alleges Castelion’s plans to build and operate the missile factory present significant risks.

The view of the storage bunkers from Sally Leber’s backyard.

It identifies a massive “storage facility fire” as the worst-case scenario. Castelion is set to store colossal stockpiles of propellants on Project Ranger’s site. The company has publicly stated its plans of working with at least one heat-sensitive chemical oxidizer—ammonium perchlorate—and storing up to 250,000 pounds inside covered magazines and bunkers, according to the Sandia report. But outside those bunkers, the report notes, Castelion is allowed to keep up to 81,000 pounds of active explosive material at a time inside the hazardous integration facilities during the assembly process.

A detonation of the facility’s largest allowed stockpile of explosive material would require a buffer from the nearest inhabited building of almost nine football fields in length. Sandia calculated that a worst-case explosion could shatter large windows as far as almost four miles away and have a 3.2 percent chance of causing minor eardrum damage to people 400 yards from the detonation.

Sandia declined to provide comment for WIRED. One of the hazard reports authors, Meg Davidson, has since left Sandia Labs and is now a senior manager of strategic partnerships at Castelion. She did not respond to requests for comment.

The report identifies a fire at the test stand as “the most probable foreseeable hazardous event.” A chemical fire would release a toxic haze containing byproducts like hydrogen chloride and chlorine. In gas form, both are highly corrosive and can cause severe eye, skin, and respiratory tract burns. Even short-term inhalation at high concentrations can cause chemical lung damage and asphyxiation.

Emails obtained through public records requests reveal that the Rio Rancho Fire Department determined 17 of the 26 buildings at the Project Ranger site would be left to burn if a fire ignited, due to the risks a chemical blaze would pose first responders. Firefighters would be barred from even entering the high-risk facilities.

Such events are hardly unheard of. WIRED reported on a June incident at Anduril’s Mississippi facility where a test run of an ammonium perchlorate–based rocket motor ended in an explosion. The blast—which didn’t cause any casualties—underscored the technical risks of scaling solid propellant production.

In May, a storage tank holding approximately 7,000 gallons of methyl methacrylate, a flammable liquid used to manufacture acrylic plastics and resins for military and commercial aircraft, began to overheat at a GKN Aerospace plant in Garden Grove, California, leading local authorities to evacuate more than 40,000 residents.

Despite these warnings, internal emails obtained through public records requests reveal that Castelion was still negotiating critical fire-response requirements between March and May—even as construction had already started.

Castelion’s site developer, Bryce Barlow, wrote to the city on March 23 that the company had not yet finalized the fire-flow response, offering to discuss the question by phone or in person instead.

After a few weeks, the requirements were replaced by city manager Matt Geisel with a statement that hard numbers would appear in the plans later on. Castelion only needed a signed memorandum of understanding to proceed—backed by the governing body’s resolution and a serviceability letter.

“Great news! Saves all of us both legal bills and headaches,” Kreitz wrote in response.

In August 2025, prior to Castelion’s formal announcement that it had selected New Mexico, Republican state senator Jay Block appeared before the Sandoval County Commission to speak in support of financing Project Ranger. Before becoming state senator, Block was a county commissioner.

The county had approved $125 million in industrial revenue bonds and had passed a resolution giving itself control over the state land picked for the site. The commissioners granted an astounding 95 percent discount on what Castelion would normally owe in property taxes. Finally, the county agreed to abate compensating taxes on out-of-state equipment, lowering the upfront costs for the incoming missile manufacturer to relocate to New Mexico.

Block’s district includes Rio Rancho as of January.

“I strongly supported Project Ranger as it moved through the local approval process, because I believed Rio Rancho and Sandoval County had the workforce, infrastructure, and defense heritage to help Castelion succeed,” Block tells WIRED.

Castelion held its first public information meeting on Project Ranger in October 2025.

“All of this happened behind the scenes. By the time it was public, it was already a done deal,” says Zachary Darden, a Rio Rancho resident who ran an unsuccessful bid for mayor on a platform opposing the development.

In November, emails show Alberto Solís, Rio Rancho’s economic development manager, coordinated Castelion CFO Kreitz’s four-day trip to New Mexico—part of the outreach process required by the state. The CFO asked to attend a Chamber of Commerce “Jingle Mingle” mixer and an event with the Sandoval Economic Alliance, a local nonprofit tasked with recruiting new business to the county.

More than a month after Kreitz’s networking trip, Castelion held its groundbreaking ceremony. Local residents weren’t invited, but many of the state’s most prominent political and business figures were. Block was there as well as US senator Martin Heinrich. So were Gregg Hull, Rio Rancho’s then mayor and Republican candidate for governor, as well as Governor Michelle Lujan Grisham. County and state economic development officials were also in attendance.

“Castelion's Project Ranger shows what a strong public-private partnership can deliver for a local community,” Rob Black, New Mexico’s secretary of economic development, tells WIRED.

Residents living next to the site, though, are contemplating whether the risks are too high to stay. But for most living in Rio Rancho Estates—a low-income neighborhood—the costs of relocation might be too high.

“This land is affordable for people who need it,” says community member Jason Clifford. “We were sold out by our local officials.”
