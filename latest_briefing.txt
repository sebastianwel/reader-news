[2] NOBELPREIS FÜR WIRTSCHAFTSWISSENSCHAFTEN FÜR US-ÖKONOMEN HALL UND WOODFORD

Die US-Amerikaner Hall und Woodford werden mit dem Wirtschaftspreis in Gedenken an Alfred Nobel ausgezeichnet. Sie werden für ihre Arbeit zur Stabilisierung der Wirtschaft geehrt, erklärte die Königlich-Schwedische Akademie der Wissenschaften.

Der Nobelpreis für Wirtschaftswissenschaften geht in diesem Jahr an Robert E. Hall und Michael Woodford aus den USA. Das gab die Königlich-Schwedische Akademie der Wissenschaften in Stockholm bekannt.

Woodford und Hall erhalten die Auszeichnung demnach für die Entwicklung makroökonomischer Modelle, die eine effektivere Stabilisierungspolitik ermöglichen. Auf Grundlage ihrer Modelle könnten gut abgestimmte fiskal- und geldpolitische Maßnahmen die wirtschaftlichen Ausschläge nach oben und unten abmildern, sagte die Generalsekretärin der Akademie, Ellen Moons, bei der Preisverkündung.

Die Preisträger hätten eine "solide theoretische Grundlage" für die Modelle geliefert, die heute von Regierungen und Zentralbanken weltweit verwendet würden, erklärte der Vorsitzende des zuständigen Komitees, John Hassler. "Mit ihnen sind wir besser gerüstet, um wirtschaftliche Schocks wie Energiekrisen oder plötzliche Veränderungen beim Konsumverhalten der Haushalte zu bewältigen."

Der Nobelpreis im Fach Wirtschaftswissenschaften wird erst seit 1969 verliehen. Er geht als einziger der Preise nicht auf das Testament des schwedischen Industriellen und Dynamit-Erfinders Alfred Nobel zurück. Der Preis wird von der schwedischen Notenbank gestiftet, deshalb nennt die Nobel-Stiftung ihn nicht offiziell Nobelpreis, sondern "Preis der Schwedischen Reichsbank für Wirtschaftswissenschaften in Gedenken an Alfred Nobel".

Dotiert ist der Preis in diesem Jahr wie die anderen Auszeichnungen auch mit zwölf Millionen schwedischen Kronen - das entspricht etwas mehr als einer Million Euro. Auch er wird am 10. Dezember, dem Todestag Nobels, verliehen.

In der vergangenen Woche waren bereits Nobelpreisträgerinnen und Nobelpreisträger in den Kategorien Medizin, Physik, Chemie, Literatur und Frieden verkündet worden.

[3] BRIEFPORTO 2027: STANDARDBRIEF KÖNNTE 1,14 EURO KOSTEN

Ein Brief für mehr als einen Euro? Das könnte bald Realität werden. Die Post soll laut der Bundesnetzagentur ihre Preise kräftig erhöhen dürfen, auch für Pakete. Was dahintersteckt und wie teuer es werden könnte.

Die Deutsche Post soll ihre Preise zum Jahreswechsel deutlich anheben dürfen. Die Bundesnetzagentur veröffentlichte einen Entwurf, nach dem das Porto für Privatkundenbriefe ab Januar 2027 im Durchschnitt um 20,35 Prozent steigen könnte.

Es wäre der bislang größte genehmigte Preisaufschlag. Auch für Pakete sind höhere Preise vorgesehen.

Jeder Ort mit mehr als 2.000 Einwohnern muss eine Postfiliale haben. Erreicht wird das nicht immer.

Wie teuer die einzelnen Briefarten werden, steht allerdings noch nicht fest. Das entscheidet die Deutsche Post später selbst. Würde sie den vorgesehenen Erhöhungsspielraum gleichmäßig auf alle Briefarten anwenden, könnte ein Standardbrief künftig 1,14 Euro kosten.

Derzeit sind es 95 Cent. Denkbar sind allerdings auch Preise von 1,10 oder 1,15 Euro, da die Post ihre Preise bislang häufig in Fünf-Cent-Schritten angepasst hat.

Damit würde das Porto für einen Standardbrief erstmals die Ein-Euro-Marke überschreiten. Seit 2012 hätte sich der Preis dann ungefähr verdoppelt. Damals kostete der Versand noch 55 Cent.

Auch Pakete für Privatkundinnen und Privatkunden könnten teurer werden - um durchschnittlich 10,72 Prozent. Für Geschäftskundenbriefe sieht die Bundesnetzagentur denselben Erhöhungsspielraum wie für Privatkunden vor: 20,35 Prozent.

Eigene Filialen der Deutschen Post gibt es fast gar nicht mehr.

Ein wesentlicher Grund für die geplante Erhöhung ist die sinkende Briefmenge. Während 2005 in Deutschland noch 16,9 Milliarden Briefe verschickt wurden, waren es 2025 nur noch knapp 9,4 Milliarden. Immer mehr Firmen und Verbraucher kommunizieren digital.

Die Kosten für die Zustellung bleiben dagegen hoch. Die Post muss weiterhin ein dichtes Netz aus Filialen, Briefkästen und Zustellpersonal unterhalten.

"Wenn die Kosten unter anderem aufgrund der zunehmenden Digitalisierung auf weniger Briefe zu verteilen sind, steigen die Preise", erklärte der Präsident der Bundesnetzagentur, Klaus Müller. Die Behörde sei bei ihrem Vorschlag unter den von der Post geforderten Preisanpassungen geblieben.

Die Deutsche Post verwies in einer ersten Reaktion auf deutlich gestiegene Kosten für Löhne, Energie und Materialien. Zugleich räumte der Konzern ein, dass zu starke Preissteigerungen die Kunden belasten und den Rückgang der Briefmengen weiter beschleunigen könnten.

"Wir werden daher mit dem von der Bundesnetzagentur eingeräumten Spielraum verantwortungsvoll umgehen", teilte die Post mit. Ziel sei eine ausgewogene Preisgestaltung, die sowohl die Wirtschaftlichkeit als auch die Interessen der Kundinnen und Kunden berücksichtige.

Dank eines Sparprogramms und einem höheren Briefporto hat die Deutsche Post ihr Ergebnis gesteigert.

Beschlossen ist die Portoerhöhung noch nicht. Zunächst können sich Unternehmen und Verbände zu dem Entwurf der Bundesnetzagentur äußern. Anschließend legt die Behörde den endgültigen Erhöhungsspielraum fest. Die Deutsche Post entscheidet dann, wie sie diesen auf die einzelnen Brief- und Paketarten verteilt. Die konkreten Preise müssen wiederum von der Bundesnetzagentur genehmigt werden.

Die neuen Portopreise sollen ab Januar 2027 gelten und für zwei Jahre Bestand haben. Im europäischen Vergleich ist der Briefversand in Deutschland bislang noch relativ günstig. In Österreich kostet ein Standardbrief einen Euro, in den Niederlanden 1,40 Euro und in Frankreich 1,52 Euro. Dort soll das Porto zum Jahreswechsel auf 1,67 Euro steigen.

[1] A NEW ANTI-AGING DRUG LOOKS TO TURN BACK THE CLOCK ON SKIN AGE

A wave of biotech companies is looking to prove that it’s possible to slow down the aging process. Mountain View, California–based Rubedo Life Sciences is starting with the most outwardly visible sign: the skin.

The company’s experimental treatment is showing early promise that it may be able to reduce the appearance of aging, according to data the company presented at the Aging Research and Drug Discovery meeting in Boston earlier this month.

In a small clinical trial, Rubedo tested its topical cream on 11 people with atopic dermatitis, an inflammatory skin condition and the most common type of eczema. The drug targets a mechanism of cellular senescence, a biological state in which damaged or stressed cells permanently stop dividing but do not die. These senescent or “zombie” cells accumulate as people age and can release substances that contribute to inflammation and tissue damage, which contributes to disease.

After four weeks of using the cream, participants lowered the biological age of their skin by about two years, on average. That estimate was based on an aging “clock” designed by a different company, Mitra Bio, which performed an independent analysis on participants’ skin samples. One person in the trial had a reduction of more than 11 years in skin age. Separately, Rubedo found that its treatment was tied to a reduction in inflammation-related markers and a 10 percent increase in a type of collagen associated with skin elasticity and structure.

Biological or epigenetic age is an estimate of how well a person is aging compared to their calendar age. Aging clocks like Mitra’s attempt to calculate this number by looking at patterns of methylations, chemical modifications to DNA that regulate how genes function and can change over time. That metric doesn’t necessarily prove that the body or a particular organ or tissue has become functionally younger, though.

“Our idea is to find ways to change the trajectory of how we age,” says Marco Quarta, Rubedo’s cofounder and chief scientific officer. “Aging is complex. There are many mechanisms, but cellular senescence is one of them.”

Senescent cells are also involved in normal biological processes, such as wound healing and tumor suppression, but their accumulation over time is associated with many age-related conditions. Getting rid of them is one of the leading strategies that anti-aging and longevity companies are pursuing.

Rubedo’s approach aims to clear out senescent cells by targeting a protein known as GPX4, a gatekeeper for cell survival. By transiently turning off GPX4, the drug is designed to make senescent cells more susceptible to ferroptosis, a regulated form of cell death, while reviving stressed, non-senescent cells nearby.

In a separate study, researchers looked at the drug’s effect on actinic keratosis, a skin condition caused by sun damage and produces rough, scaly skin patches and can sometimes progress to skin cancer. In a trial of 18 people, the cream led to a 46 percent reduction in total lesions after a month of use.

Rubedo is studying the same drug for psoriasis and skin with sun damage. Soon, the company will add androgenetic alopecia, a type of hereditary hair loss, to the list. CEO Frederick Beddingfield, previously the chief medical officer at Allergan, where he launched Botox, sees potential in a lower-potency, over-the-counter version for general skin aging, which would compete with retinol. The company is also formulating the drug into a pill to study whether it can be used to treat metabolic conditions associated with aging.

“It's great to see them doing this work,” says Eric Verdin, president and CEO of the Buck Institute for Research on Aging, which has developed several aging clocks. “I would probably be a little circumspect about what these clocks really measure, because right now, there are a proliferation of them.”

Plus, the results that patients will care about is how their skin actually looks and feels, not a result from a biological aging test.

And senescence is just one piece of the aging puzzle, says Anthony Rossi, a dermatologist at Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center. “Some senescent cells are good because they turn things off, like cancer progression.”

He says the science is novel but the work is still very early-stage, and it will be interesting to see how it affects biological aging in people with healthy skin, not just those with certain skin conditions. “The best anti-aging routine right now is sleep, a good diet, and exercise,” he says. For now, at least.

[2] FAR-RIGHT ELECTION GROUPS ARE NOW TARGETING NURSING HOMES

With just weeks to go until the midterms, election denial groups have found a new target: nursing homes.

The groups, led by the right-wing Election Integrity Network, have claimed without evidence that left-wing organizations are perpetrating voter fraud inside nursing homes, care homes, and assisted-living facilities. The network is now recruiting their followers to contact and question those facilities.

“These left-wing groups go into the nursing homes, and they go into the group homes, and they basically steal the votes of these most vulnerable,” Cleta Mitchell, the Election Integrity Network’s founder, said in a recent appearance on Steve Bannon’s War Room show.

As evidence, Mitchell referenced a case in Wisconsin from 2020 in which a sheriff alleged that eight residents in a care home had their votes filled out for them. The Wisconsin Election Commission rejected the claims. Mitchell told Bannon the case could have cost president Donald Trump the 2020 election, though that wouldn’t be possible: Trump lost Wisconsin in 2020 by over 20,000 votes.

Mitchell’s group has been urging followers to download a toolkit from the Election Integrity Network website and email and call nursing homes in their area. The group has also created multiple images for followers to share on social media which reinforce the idea of widespread voter fraud at nursing homes.

In one campaign, the network shared a series of posts that mapped residential care facilities in competitive congressional districts in states including Arizona, California, New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, and Virginia. The posts identified “vulnerable residents” who “may become targets for political predators seeking easy access to ballots.”

Mitchell and the Election Integrity Network did not respond to requests for comment.

The effort is part of a much broader push by election deniers to undermine trust in the midterms. And just like the claims of noncitizen voting and widespread mail-in voter fraud, they have been supercharged by the Trump administration.

In July, the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) issued a memo that removed previous guidance on how staff at nursing homes could help residents to vote, such as assisting with mail-in voting or transporting residents to polling locations. The new memo replaced that guidance with threats of prosecution if staff “engage in inappropriate coercion or other violations of residents’ rights.”

The memo also claimed that the agency has “become aware of several allegations of instances in which resident rights were unlawfully violated.” However, the examples cited in the memo did not lead to any proven cases of voter fraud. CMS did not respond to a request for comment.

In August, senators led by Elizabeth Warren and Alex Padilla slammed the changes, writing in a letter to CMS that rather than protecting the country’s 2 million nursing home residents, the new rules would disenfranchise them.

“CMS issued a memo that appears to be an attempt to intimidate nursing home staff from assisting residents in the voting process,” the senators wrote in the letter, addressed to CMS administrator Mehmet Oz (who himself has flip-flopped on whether the 2020 election was rigged).

But now, with less than a month to go before the midterms, groups like the Election Integrity Network—which has close ties to the White House—are pushing the narrative that nursing homes are a hive of vote rigging activity.

Another election denial group, the New York–based Project Civicia, has launched a similar campaign, entitled “Adopt a Care Facility.” The group told WIRED that it could not share how many people were taking part in the campaign but repeated the claims made by CMS about voter fraud in Wisconsin, in addition to other allegations.

“We do not presume wrongdoing by a facility because its residents need voting assistance,” Kim Hermance, cofounder and president of Project Civica, tells WIRED. “There are other public cases of forged absentee applications and similar problems. Separately, in our own review of New York records, we noticed people over 85 who appeared to register and vote for the first time shortly before an election. That is not illegal by itself, but it raised a basic question about how facilities handle the process.”

[3] THEY WAR-GAMED DIMMING THE SUN. IT WENT EVEN WORSE THAN YOU THINK

When it comes to deciding the fate of the planet, Colleen Golja always thought science and reason would have a seat at the table. That was until one morning last September in London, when the climate researcher took part in the first in a series of war games. The goal: to prevent an uncontrolled global race to develop a technology aimed at cooling the planet but that comes with vast, unknown risks that could reshape life on Earth.

“I thought that would be such an easy thing. Like, we can all agree to do ethical research and make sure it's for the better of all humans,” she told WIRED. “And boy, was I wrong.”

The simulation, titled Exploring Geopolitical Tipping Points and Climate Cooling Strategies, saw a couple dozen foreign-relations experts, scientists, military personnel, and former government officials from across the world convene in a meeting room at Queen Mary University of London. After making introductions over coffee and little yogurts, they sat at tables arranged in a square with the name of the country or organization each person was playing: national governments, climate NGOs and nonprofits, indigenous groups, and tech companies. Golja was assigned to provide technical advice to help participants make scientifically literate decisions.

The point of the game was to stress-test the world’s response to a group of prospective technologies known collectively as solar radiation modification. These technologies promise to reverse global warming by reflecting away a portion of the sunlight reaching Earth, for example by spraying particles of sulphur dioxide or some other manufactured substance into the atmosphere. The cooling effect of this kind of solar geoengineering is widely accepted. It would mimic the well-known impacts of large volcanic eruptions, which temporarily cool the planet by half a degree or more. But the fallout is less certain—and less equal. Some regions will suffer: Models predict drying in sub-Saharan Africa and more intense tropical storms in the Atlantic. The cooling effect would also last only as long as the geoengineering continued; after that the global temperature could shoot back up with wild and hard-to-predict effects.

Until recently, the idea of solar radiation modification was a speculative niche of climate science. That has changed fast. Private firms are exploring solar geoengineering as a commercial opportunity: Last year, a US-Israeli startup called Stardust Solutions raised $60 million, and in May it released details of a suite of new inventions it claims would give humans the ability to dial down the global temperature as desired. In August, Elon Musk, whose company SpaceX might be a candidate for delivering geoengineering interventions, threw his voice behind solar geoengineering, writing on X: “Space satellites to control temperature and massive geo-engineering will be needed or it will be game over.”

The world is going to have to make decisions about how to govern this technology much sooner than most policymakers imagine, Cynthia Scharf, a former UN advisor, told WIRED. “It's not 15 years from now; it's in the next decade, easily.”

It was against this backdrop that Danielle Young, an assistant professor in international relations at Leeds University, arranged the war game. Its setting was a hastily convened UN committee charged with coordinating the international response to developments in solar geoengineering. The game unfolded over simulated meetings set at three points in time: the present, September 2027, and September 2028. Each step ratcheted up the stakes, with the game creators introducing new advances in sunlight-reflecting technology. As in real life, no one knew all the facts, and players had to respond to events that were bringing the world closer to a full-scale attempt at deploying solar geoengineering tech, even when they may not have a full picture of what had truly occurred—or what any of the others were up to.

The first session set the tone. Distrust and disinformation dominated. As participants discussed rules governing outdoor experiments in an attempt to agree an international deal, players representing both the US and China told the group they could not sign a deal that constrained their pursuit of research considered to be in their “national interest.” Nor could they accept a deal that would see their superpower rival taking the lead technologically. Speaking to WIRED, Jeffrey Nielsen, a major in the US Army who played the role of a US national security attaché, summarized the position as “worse than no deal at all is any deal that favors China.”

Tensions escalated when the team running the simulation handed the Russian delegates an “intelligence report” indicating they had found a plane near the Bering Strait believed to be involved in a US geoengineering research program. The US team—which was indeed flying research missions—accused Russia of disinformation.

Meanwhile, the US became convinced China was also conducting research and developing its own program. Beijing’s representatives had tried to insert language into a mooted deal that would ensure no retroactive punishment for past experiments. Then, China approached a player representing SpaceX to float the idea of a technology-sharing program. In a private discussion away from the group, the SpaceX player immediately reported the Chinese approach to the US team. The US demanded an international investigation. The finger-pointing continued, and countries abandoned the talks. The Canadian representative called it a “humiliating defeat.”

At the end of the session, Nielsen was pumped. He’d busted China and derailed any prospect of a global deal. In the context of the game, he told WIRED, it was a “humungous victory” for the Americans. But Young, the researcher who designed the scenario, said the Chinese were not, in fact, conducting experiments in the game. Mutual suspicion meant all parties left the room believing the others were moving as quickly as possible to build their own geoengineering capability.

What played out in the first session reflected “the likelihood, in my opinion, of the geoengineering arms race,” says Nielsen, who clarified he was speaking to WIRED in a private capacity and not representing the views of the actual US government. Most experts agree it would take a superpower’s reach to ensure the safe passage of planes needed to deliver payloads of dust to the stratosphere in a geoengineering exercise. But neither the US nor China would lightly cede control over the global thermostat, says Nielsen, and distrust and poor communication are a significant risk: “Any action related to the issue by one actor can be perceived as malintent by the other.”

The threat of falling behind a rival is “a very strong driver of research and development of capability,” a former British diplomat with a background in national security told WIRED. “But that's not at all the same thing as deployment.” There are similarities with nuclear arms dynamics—though solar radiation management, unlike nukes, has more obvious benefits as well as downsides, many of which are poorly understood. “There is an interdependence here,” says the former diplomat, who was not involved in the game and spoke on condition of anonymity to preserve relationships. “It's not an inherently zero-sum game.”

Scharf, who represented the US government alongside Nielsen in the game, told WIRED a “scientifically transparent international collaboration” between China and the US “might be the best we can do in terms of fending off a very dangerous competition between the two. But I certainly cannot imagine that happening under the Trump administration.”

Compounding the problem is a lack of information. Geoengineering has historically been viewed with suspicion and often ignored by the scientific mainstream. One researcher told WIRED he’d experienced ostracization and barriers to academic progression owing to his interest in the technology; he no longer works in the field.

This lack of scientific interest greatly frustrated Golja, who spent much of the game watching as a bystander while political posturing dominated. Given the low level of knowledge around solar radiation modification and its side effects, she was one of very few experts who could help the participants understand the climate impacts of what they were discussing—yet she was largely ignored.“I was just so surprised about how little anyone actually cared about the science,” she says.

In the next sessions, the organizers tried in vain to nudge the talks back toward the realities of climate change. The participants received frequent notifications of climate disasters. These barely registered. Golja had prepared an “emulator,” a real-world demonstration of the best available science on how solar radiation management might affect weather patterns in different regions. It was ignored. If country representatives asked her anything, it was usually a leading question looking for data that would serve their ends or frame a rival’s proposal as reckless.

Then, during the last day, players were trying to find a way to respond to reports that a major plume of aerosols had been detected. In the game, this had been caused by a joint US-Saudi test of Stardust’s technology, though not everyone knew this. Finally, scientific advice was at a premium. Countries needed to know how to establish a monitoring network of satellites and other observational tools in order to prevent tests like this from happening in secret. A player representing an NGO called the Climate Futures Cooperative approached Golja, offering her money to fund such a scheme.

“Great. Fabulous. Why wouldn't I do that?” she recalls thinking.

But it soon came out that the Climate Futures Cooperative was actually backed by the fossil fuel industry. The organizers had recognized that fossil fuel companies and major producing countries like the US and Saudi Arabia would have a clear interest in removing barriers from the development of a technology that could combat climate change without having to immediately stop burning coal, oil, and gas. The shadowy NGO spent the game subtly wrecking the vibe and carping about process—though mostly they just sat back and let it burn. Some players—Russia, the US, SpaceX and Stardust—figured out the CFC was not what it seemed, says Young. “But they assessed it was to their benefit and didn’t draw attention to it.” Then the CFC chose its moment to offer money to Golja, only to crater her credibility, isolating and fragmenting the voice of science in the room.

That final lesson in fossil fuel realpolitik was about all Golja could endure. By the end of the last day, she was in tears.

At no point did the countries in the game agree to a deal, despite multiple proposals being put forward. European countries and Canada tried to build support for safeguards and open scientific research, but the talks constantly got bogged down by delay tactics and veered into tense showdowns over duelling strategic interests. In their final report, the researchers running the simulation—whose work was sponsored by the UK government-funded Advanced Research and Invention Agency—concluded that “strategic competition and state sovereignty drove the simulations, not climate concerns.”

Simulations like this one are not supposed to predict the future. The outcomes depend on the design of the game and the relative personalities and experience of the players. Many of those in the room lacked first-hand diplomatic and national security experience. The UN forum, which organizers selected as the game’s setting, may also not be as effective as talks between smaller groups of major countries. But the experiment in London echoed and refracted through the real world, during and after the months it was running.

In March, two papers from conservative US think tanks urged the Trump administration to take the lead on solar radiation modification and counter China’s threat. One memorably invoked a new, MAGA-coded term: “Weather sovereignty.” This month, the Observer newspaper in London revealed that the UK Ministry of Defense had paid a deep tech security consultancy to conduct research into sunlight reflection. Then, last week, the British government released its first official policy paper on the field, backing further research but stating firmly that it did not support deployment and “does not plan to do so.” Stardust has continued its lobbying of the US and governments around the world; CEO Yanai Yedvab told WIRED its job was getting easier as, unlike before, “the majority of policymakers … are already aware of this technology.”

Stardust has always maintained that it will not conduct large-scale outdoor experiments, let alone full deployment, before global governance is in place. “We are developing a very powerful tool,” Yedvab says. “When you develop this kind of powerful technology—and you have many precedents—national interests will play out. We're not naive about it.”

Meanwhile, drivers of the climate crisis are intensifying. During the time span of the game, efforts to curtail the use of fossil fuels were being undermined by rising energy costs and political hostility. Week after week, an incredible summer of drought and heat pounded Europe. In the final week of August, the collapse of a glacier on the Tibet-Nepal border fulfilled the prophecies of decades of climate scientists, killing more than 1,450 people.

In the months since the simulation ended, Golja has reevaluated many things. On a corkboard behind her desk, she has pinned up a note to herself, laying out a vision for life in a world ruled by narrow geopolitical self-interest rather than science, evidence, and reason. Her answer: She plans to return to the small coastal town where she grew up in Connecticut and use her expertise to help local climate groups build the seawalls and coastal erosion buffers they will need to cope with the inevitable. “To try and make a difference where it feels like I actually have traction,” she says. After what she saw, pursuing science in the hope of informing global decisions no longer held as much appeal. “Now it feels a little bit more personal.”
