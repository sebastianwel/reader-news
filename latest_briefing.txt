[1] US-EINWANDERUNGSBEHÖRDE ICE MELDET REKORD BEI ABSCHIEBUNGEN

Fast eine halbe Million Menschen hat die US-Regierung binnen einen Jahres - laut eigenen Angaben. Das soll ein neuer Rekordwert sein. Es gibt aber Zweifel an der Transparenz der Daten.

Die US-Einwanderungsbehörde ICE hat laut eigenen Angaben im vergangenen Haushaltsjahr so viele Menschen abgeschoben wie nie zuvor in ihrer 23-jährigen Geschichte. Laut Heimatschutzministerium wurden bis Ende September 483.200 Menschen durch die ICE außer Landes gebracht.

Die Daten des Ministeriums zeigen einen erheblichen Anstieg der Abschiebezahlen seit Trumps Amtsantritt. Während es in den beiden Jahren, die ganz oder mehrheitlich in seine Amtszeit fielen, weit mehr als 400.000 Abschiebungen gab, waren es im letzten Jahr unter seinem Vorgänger Joe Biden 271.000 gewesen.

Die brachialen ICE-Einsätze wühlen die USA weiterhin auf. Was darf die Einwanderungsbehörde - und was nicht?

Allerdings äußern Experten Zweifel an der Transparenz der Daten. Denn Einzelheiten - zum Beispiel wie viele der Abgeschobenen Vorstrafen haben - sind nicht öffentlich bekannt. Außerdem stellt die Regierung seit Beginn von Trumps zweiter Amtszeit keine regelmäßigen detaillierten Statistiken zur Einwanderungshaft und zu Abschiebungen mehr bereit.

Trump hatte die Abschiebepolitik der USA nach Amtsantritt erheblich verschärft. Die Regierung stellte zusätzliche Mittel für Abschiebungsbeamte, Haftanstalten und Abschiebeflüge bereit.

[2] WAS ÜBER DEN AUSFALL VON MICROSOFT-DIENSTEN BEKANNT IST

Weltweit sind seit vielen Stunden Dienste des US-Softwarekonzerns Microsoft ausgefallen oder gestört, unter anderem das E-Mail-Programm Outlook. Wer davon betroffen ist und welche Hintergründe vermutet werden - ein Überblick.

Microsoft-Dienste weltweit sind von einer massiven Störung betroffen - und zwar bereits seit Donnerstag. Unternehmen und Behörden haben große Probleme mit wichtiger Software, ebenso wie private Nutzer. Maßnahmen zur Behebung des Problems sind laut Microsoft "nicht wie erhofft" verlaufen.

Durch einen großflächigen Ausfall von Microsoft-Diensten, darunter Outlook, kommt es weltweit zu Störungen.

Betroffen sind nicht nur das E-Mail-Programm Outlook, sondern generell Dienste aus dem Cloud-Paket Microsoft 365. Beim Bürokommunikations-Angebot Teams etwa funktionierten das Planen von Besprechungen und Kalender-Anbindungen häufig nicht richtig.

Zudem waren der KI-Assistent Copilot, das Verwaltungsportal für Administratoren sowie Schnittstellen zu Drittanbietern - darunter KI-Dienste wie OpenAI und Anthropic - mindestens zeitweise gestört.

Hinweise auf einen Cyberangriff gibt es nach Angaben von Microsoft nicht. Der australische Innen- und Cybersicherheitsminister Tony Burke erklärte, der Konzern gehe nicht von einem Cyberangriff aus. Vielmehr handele es sich offenbar um ein fehlerhaftes Update, ähnlich wie bei der weltweiten IT-Panne durch die Sicherheitsfirma CrowdStrike im Jahr 2024.

Ein mutmaßlicher Hackerangriff auf Rheinmetall legt vertrauliche Informationen offen.

Das ist unklar. Die massive Störung beim US-Technologiekonzern hatte bereits am Donnerstag Zehntausenden Nutzern Probleme beim Zugriff auf ihre E-Mails in Outlook bereitet. Wie die Störungsplattform Downdetector berichtete, weitete sich die Störung binnen einer Stunde weltweit aus und legte Microsoft-Dienste in mehr als 70 Ländern lahm. Bekannt ist, dass weltweit rund 430 Millionen Menschen die Dienste von Microsoft 365 nutzen.

Sensible Regierungsbereiche sind ebenfalls von der Störung betroffen. Etwa der Geheimdienst und die Bundespolizei in Australien meldeten Ausfälle. Die Regierung in Canberra aktivierte ein Abkommen mit Microsoft, um eine vorrangige Wiederherstellung der Systeme sicherzustellen. ​

[3] ERLEICHTERUNG FÜR INDUSTRIE IM EU-EMISSIONSHANDEL

Die Industrie in der EU soll weniger für Emissionen zahlen müssen. Unterhändler von EU-Parlament und Mitgliedstaaten einigten sich darauf, betroffenen Branchen zeitlich befristet zusätzliche kostenlose Emissionszertifikate zuzuteilen.

Durch eine zeitlich begrenzte Veränderung im Emissionshandelssystem der EU soll die Industrie nach Angaben des Europaparlaments mehrere Milliarden Euro einsparen können. Unterhändler des Parlaments und der EU-Staaten einigten sich in der Nacht darauf, der Industrie für den Zeitraum von 2026 bis 2030 rund 120 Millionen zusätzliche kostenlose EU-Emissionshandelszertifikate zuzuteilen.

Damit gehen die Verhandler noch über den Vorschlag der EU-Kommission hinaus, mit dem die betroffenen Branchen nach Angaben der Behörde rund sechs Milliarden Euro eingespart hätten. Da die Vereinbarung nun noch mehr kostenlose Zertifikate vorsieht, steigen laut Parlament die erwarteten Einsparungen. Der Kompromiss muss noch vom Parlament und von den Mitgliedstaaten formell angenommen werden.

Die EU-Kommission will ihr wichtigstes Klimaschutz-Instrument aufweichen. Die Reaktionen sind gespalten.

Die EU-Kommission hatte im Juli weitere Anpassungen des Emissionshandelssystems vorgeschlagen, um die europäische Industrie angesichts hoher Energiepreise und Konkurrenz aus dem Ausland zu unterstützen und Abwanderung zu vermeiden. Demnach soll etwa die Zahl der CO2-Zertifikate langsamer sinken als bislang vorgesehen. Das würde bedeuten, dass die Industrie in Europa länger und mehr Kohlendioxid ausstoßen darf. Die Klimaziele sind damit schwieriger zu erreichen. Eigentlich will die EU bis 2040 ihre Treibhausgasemissionen um 90 Prozent im Vergleich zu 1990 senken.

[1] ‘CALL HIM OUT’: CYBERTRUCK LOYALISTS ARE IN MOURNING—AND THEY WANT ANSWERS FROM ELON MUSK

Last week, Elon Musk announced on X that Tesla would halt production of the Cybertruck at Gigafactory Texas “around the middle of next year,” supposedly to allow for increased production of self-driving Cybercabs.

Automotive industry observers were quick to surmise that the vehicle’s weak sales, delayed deliveries, inflated pricing, numerous recalls, and connection to Musk’s far-right MAGA politics may also have factored into the decision. Longtime detractors of the polarizing steel-paneled truck, originally positioned as an electric rival to classic gas-powered pickups, jeered Musk and Tesla fans over its demise, noting with some pleasure that the Cybertruck would exit the market within four years of its November 2023 rollout.

And, in Cybertruck fan communities across the web, owners began to mourn.

“Worst possible news,” a member of the Cybertruck Owners Club forum wrote in a response to Musk’s post. “No chance to buy another in future years for a newer model or after a car accident. No more aftermarket mods. No more service or parts.” Another remarked that they had waited for their truck “longer than the truck line was around.”

“So everybody in my group, they are not happy,” Michael Goldman, a contractor in Palm Springs, California, tells WIRED. As the administrator of the Facebook group Cybertruck Owners Only, which has more than 82,000 members, he’s been swamped with frantic messages. “I've had people reaching out to me through the group, asking me, like, ‘What are you going to do?’” Goldman says. “And I'm like, ‘Dude, I don't know.’”

Since hearing the news about the discontinuation of the truck, many owners have expressed feelings of betrayal and disappointment. Some are wondering if their pending orders will be fulfilled, as Musk has claimed that Tesla “will have to return deposits for a lot of people” due to demand outpacing production. Others have looked for a silver lining, speculating that the Cybertruck could become a valued collector’s item.

But Goldman doesn’t want to turn his truck into a “garage princess,” since he enjoys driving it and finds it useful in his construction business. “I don't want to ever drive another truck,” he says, and he considered buying a backup Cybertruck for when his first one reaches the end of its life. “I'll be honest with you, I was going pull the trigger on a [Cyberbeast] about two days ago, and I have a reservation already in my phone,” Goldman says, but when he was ready to complete the purchase, “the price increased like another $8,000 or something, and that was my sign to not do it.”

That Musk, against all odds, actually brought his vision of the Cybertruck to life makes it all the more painful for Goldman to see it scrapped. “That it actually happened and only happened for such a short period of time kind of blows me away,” he says. “That's why I want to call him out on it, you know?”

Troy Meekhof, a Tesla aficionado in Grand Rapids, Michigan, known as “the Cybertruck Guy” on social media, has not been shy about expressing his own displeasure. “I can’t help but think this is Tesla’s own unwillingness (or incompetence) to make Cybertruck a success,” he posted in reply to Musk’s announcement of the wind-down last week. “Where was the price cuts? Where were the advertisements? Where is range extender?”

“I absolutely do think this is the wrong move for the Tesla brand, and it’s not necessarily because of the removal of an under-performing product, but it’s a clear symbol of Tesla’s aggression towards transitioning to autonomous vehicles only,” such as the two-seater Cybercab, Meekhof tells WIRED in an email. “If you ask me, it is far too aggressive, and I have a growing conviction that, at least in America, the population and legislators are not ready nor interested in autonomous vehicles today en masse.”

In another X post, Meekhof warned that “Tesla has lost the plot” by going all-in on autonomous driving as the US enters “a governance era that opposes” the company’s efforts on that front.

Musk has repeatedly declared that Tesla’s future is as an AI and robotics firm rather than as an automaker. It’s a strategy that has increasingly alienated Tesla owners like Meekhof, who despite their excitement over self-driving technology, feel that the company is spurning its core supporters as it pursues breakthroughs in that field. Earlier this year, Tesla axed its Model S and Model X, and the end of the Cybertruck narrows its lineup to just the Model 3 and Model Y.

“Tesla, as a company, has made itself massively successful by making vehicles that appeal to enthusiasts, in either tech or automotive, and in many cases, both,” Meekhof says. “With their transition to autonomous-only vehicles, they leave those customers behind. I am one of them.” Meekhof has launched what he describes as a mostly “symbolic” petition urging Tesla to reconsider the plan to eliminate the Cybertruck from its slate. The letter has gathered more than 5,000 signatures.

Drew Million, an active member of the Cybertruck Owners Only group on Facebook, recently shared on the page that he was concerned about the transition to another vehicle once his beloved Cybertruck has run its course. “I’ve lost a lot of love for Tesla over this,” Million tells WIRED, adding that his interest in the brand is waning due to the “very stale consumer options” left on the table.

Million had ordered a second Cybertruck in February to replace a work van, and he’s hoping that it comes through before production halts completely in June or July 2027, per Musk’s estimate. But he’s dismayed, he says, that Tesla leadership isn’t paying attention to what customers actually want.

“After Cybertruck is gone, there isn’t a flagship vehicle,” Million says, nor one that meets his needs. His household went from owning no electric cars to four in less than a year. “The Cybertruck started that,” he says, and it can’t be replaced by rideshares in autonomous taxis.

It’s a point that comes up again and again from Tesla boosters protesting the sunset of the Cybertruck: They don’t want to give up their own independence in favor of robot chauffeurs. An owner wrote on X, for example, that they didn’t understand Tesla’s push “to delete the steering wheel, the pedals, the ownership, the freedom of having a vehicle” with the Cybercab, which cannot accommodate an entire family. They lamented that the company “doesn't seem to want to continue innovating in the customer car segment.”

Meekhof predicts that Americans won’t soon give up personally controlled trucks and SUVs capable of transporting several people and cargo in favor of smaller autonomous sedans. He worries that Tesla is headed for a “Google Glass scenario on steroids, where the technology came before the people.” Goldman is on the same page. “I think a Cybercab is cool for certain things, but I'm not going to take a Cybercab to a construction site, you know what I mean?” he says. “In rural towns, people want to drive their car to work.”

The overall consternation in Tesla world over the fate of Cybertruck has been somewhat compounded by Musk’s typically muddled messaging. According to the CEO, the truck is profitable and, indeed, so popular that the Gigafactory can’t make them fast enough to fulfill existing reservations—so why, owners ask, does it have to be suspended?

Would-be customers have also been disgruntled to learn that Tesla continues to accept Cybertruck orders, though only for the premium Cyberbeast model, priced at $109,990. “So they can't fill current orders, but will still take orders on the most expensive version ... yeah that's not scummy at all,” a redditor griped this week on r/cybertruck.

And there is a percolating sense of resentment among some Cybertruck devotees that they were taken for a ride, so to speak, with Tesla exploiting their passion for the novel vehicle and its driver-assistance tech to advance its agenda of autonomy at all costs.

“They're a data company,” Goldman says. “What they're doing is collecting data for the robots and for the self-driving cars and for the Cybercab. We were basically beta testers in the Cybertruck for the Cybercab.”

That’s part of the reason that Goldman and others feel they are owed a better explanation of Tesla’s latest move. “We want to know why,” Goldman says. “Is it because we were really just beta for the cabs, or is it really because you guys don't have enough space in the Gigafactory, or is it just because the sales are terrible?”

“Most of the people that have Cybertrucks have been following this truck for the past 10 years,” Goldman says. “We finally got literally the best truck made on this planet, and now they're taking it away from us, and we're bummed.”

[2] THE HUBBLE SPACE TELESCOPE IS SINKING. NASA IS LOOKING FOR WAYS TO KEEP IT AFLOAT

As telescopes go, few if any are more famous than the Hubble Space Telescope. Over 30 years of operations, the telescope has been essential to groundbreaking discoveries that have fundamentally transformed humanity’s view of the universe. But like all satellites in low Earth orbit, it’s been gradually descending.

This happens because of atmospheric drag, and the Hubble Space Telescope is not equipped with a propulsion system to adjust its own orbit. According to NASA’s analysis, if left unchecked, the telescope will likely re-enter Earth’s atmosphere in 2033 or 2034, eventually crashing back onto the planet.

Hoping to delay this conclusion, NASA has issued a formal Request for Information seeking proposals from the private sector for “in-space servicing” of the famous telescope. This would likely involve raising Hubble’s altitude to extend its operational lifespan, but the agency is also open to industry’s suggestions for managing a safe and controlled reentry or disposal of the telescope while still in orbit. Space companies can also propose upgrades to enhance the telescope’s functionality in its remaining years.

“Hubble is a national icon,” Nicky Fox, associate administrator of NASA's Science Mission Directorate, said in a statement. “Given the time we have until Hubble’s anticipated reentry, we are eager to explore the breadth of possibilities to boost Hubble to a higher orbit while we develop the flagship mission to follow.”

The telescope was designed with the expectation that astronauts would perform repairs and upgrades in orbit. Since it was launched in 1990, a total of five service missions have been carried out. The last of these missions was canceled after the space shuttle Columbia disaster in 2003. But NASA later determined that the benefits of servicing Hubble outweighed the risks of a final mission, and in 2009 a crew took measures, such as replacing the battery, to extend the telescope’s lifespan.

This is also not the first time NASA has considered private-sector resources for raising the Hubble Space Telescope’s orbit. In September 2022, NASA entered into a nonfunding partnership agreement with SpaceX to investigate the possibility of using the company’s Dragon spacecraft to move Hubble into a more stable orbit. However, according to NASA’s announcement at the time, the agency had no plans to conduct or fund the service mission itself; the study was intended solely to assess commercial feasibility.

Meanwhile, NASA commissioned the private company Katalyst Space Technologies in September 2025 to perform an orbital raise for the Swift Observatory, which was experiencing faster-than-expected orbital decay. However, the company’s spacecraft, launched in July 2026, suffered an attitude control malfunction, forcing the mission to be abandoned. On November 4, Swift reentered the atmosphere over Indonesia.

The problems facing the Hubble Space Telescope are not limited to its drop in altitude. The spacecraft itself is also experiencing malfunctions. In June 2024, NASA announced that it would transition the Hubble Space Telescope to a new operating mode using only one of the six gyros on board to assess the telescope’s orientation. After more than three decades in space, only three of the gyros were operational, and one of those was starting to return abnormal readings. The other remaining functional gyro is now being kept in a ready state for future use.

NASA estimates that a mission to raise the telescope’s orbit will need to be carried out by 2032. In an announcement made in 2022, NASA also explained that if the Hubble Space Telescope could be raised to a higher, more stable orbit, it could continue to observe the cosmos for several more years.

“Hubble still has important science ahead of it, and we should be looking at every reasonable opportunity to keep it delivering for as long as possible,” said NASA administrator Jared Isaacman. “If there are innovative and cost-effective ways to extend the life of an asset already in orbit, we owe it to the American taxpayer and the scientific community to explore them. Extending Hubble’s life would mean more discoveries, more science, and more opportunities to expand our understanding of the universe.”

[3] BITDEFENDER ANTIVIRUS PLUS REVIEW: UNDERCUTTING THE COMPETITION

Antivirus programs are a dime a dozen these days, and while the vast array of security threats that assail modern devices can at times feel so overwhelming that protection seems impossible, a good antivirus solution remains essential.

Unfortunately, purchasing an antivirus can prove rather confusing. Companies offer a huge range of bundles with long lists of features, many of which are obscured in vague language. If you dig deep enough into a company’s website, however, you’ll find that most still offer the option to purchase the antivirus with few add-ons at a lower price. And if that’s what you want, Bitdefender’s Antivirus Plus is a good option.

Buying Bitdefender Antivirus Plus on its own is arguably a bit confusing, as the company strongly pushes shoppers toward more expensive and all-inclusive Bitdefender bundles, which include other features like a password manager, dark web monitoring, and lots more. In total, there are four different bundles to choose from: Total Security, Premium Security, Ultimate Security, and Ultimate Security Plus Extended, ranging from $60 up to $140 for the first year. There are also Family versions of these plans, which are more expensive but add protection for up to 25 devices and parental controls. All these bundles include the same antivirus protection, but if you search for Antivirus Plus on its own, though, you will find it. And it’s arguably worth seeking out, as Bitdefender's entry-level options are priced most aggressively.

Bitdefender Antivirus Plus currently offers an introductory price of $25 for the first year (and $50 after) for a single device or $30 for the first year ($70 after) for three devices. This is quite a bit less than Bitdefender Total Security, which is $60 for the first year (and $110 after) and only offers two benefits in exchange: a password manager and protection for up to five devices instead of just one. The lower tiers tend to be better deals, especially if you're after something specific.

That’s because Bitdefender’s pricing is most competitive at its lowest tiers, which may by itself be enough reason for many to choose it. Rivals like Norton Antivirus Standard, McAfee Essential, Avira Internet Security, and Malwarebytes Standard typically ask more for a similar level of service.

There is some variation here; Norton throws in a password manager at a lower price point, for instance, and some competitors have superior VPN service included (Bitdefender Antivirus Plus limits you to 200 MB of VPN data per day, which isn’t much). Still, Bitdefender Antivirus Plus is among the more affordable options, particularly if you’re more interested in the antivirus than the add-ons.

Savvy users might balk at paying at all, as many free antivirus options exist. Bitdefender itself offers a free antivirus download for both Windows and Android (but not macOS, iOS, or iPadOS). This is a fair choice, though it often means you’ll have to tolerate advertisements urging you to sign up.

Buying, downloading, and installing Bitdefender Antivirus Plus is a smooth experience. The payment portal is straightforward. The installer is really a downloader, so it’s a small file; the full download required by the installer is roughly 800 megabytes. Once the installation is finished, Antivirus Plus automatically runs an initial virus check. This took less than five minutes on a Microsoft Surface Laptop 7 running Windows 11.

Once installed, the Bitdefender Antivirus Plus interface provides a default dashboard that includes several common actions, including a quick scan, total system scan, and vulnerability scan (the latter looks for problems like a weak account password or an overly permissive network configuration). You’ll also find an option to turn on Bitdefender VPN, which is installed alongside the antivirus—though, as mentioned, you’re limited to 200 MB of data per day unless you upgrade to Bitdefender Premium VPN.

These features are largely standard for an antivirus program, though one extra feature requires more explanation: Safepay. This creates a sandboxed browsing environment meant to thwart threats like keyloggers and man-in-the-middle attacks. To be honest, I’m doubtful of the feature’s utility. I don’t believe these are the most common avenues cybercriminals use to obtain payment information; they’re far more likely to buy huge repositories of payment data stolen from compromised companies. It’s also rather clunky to launch Safepay for an online purchase, so I don’t think most people will want to use it.

I spent most of my time using Bitdefender Antivirus Plus on Windows, which I think is the best version of the software. The Android and iOS versions look largely similar to it, though of course compressed for a mobile device. However, macOS is the odd one out with an interface that looks different from its peers and, to my eyes, seems about five years out of date. It’s not terrible and has similar functionality, but I find this a bit odd. It seems like the macOS software is missing a software refresh that other platforms received.

A nice software interface is a perk but, at its core, antivirus software is supposed to stop viruses and other malware. So, how does Bitdefender Antivirus Plus rank?

Bitdefender scored well in AV-Test’s latest report, where it detected 100 percent of the tested attacks and received an AV-Test Top Product certification. It also received high marks from AV-Comparatives, which found it detected 99.5 percent of threats, enough to earn it the top three-star score. Bitdefender’s consumer products weren’t ranked in the latest SE Labs consumer antivirus report, but the small-business product detected 99 percent of threats and received an AAA ranking in the April 2026 report.

The performance hit from Bitdefender Antivirus Plus wasn’t noticeable in typical use of either my Windows 11 laptop or my Apple MacBook Air M4. Bitdefender services consumed about 700 MB of memory in total on both platforms. That’s not a lot, and it's in line with what I often see from antivirus programs, though it’s also not trivial. I can imagine this could at times result in a performance hit on a device with 8 GB of RAM. My devices have 32 GB and 24 GB, respectively, however, so I didn’t encounter any issues.

Bitdefender Antivirus Plus checks off most boxes for an antivirus program. It’s affordable, easy to use, earns high marks from malware testing labs, and avoids significant performance issues when installed. But I do want to return to a problem I’ve mentioned before: the VPN.

Antivirus Plus advertises an included VPN. This is true, but it’s actually the free VPN service that anyone can obtain. The service has a strict 200-MB (per day, per device) data cap, which isn’t going to be enough if you want to use it as your main VPN. To be fair, Bitdefender does detail this limitation on its website—but it does seem like an added feature when, in reality, it’s available for free whether you buy Antivirus Plus or not.

You can, of course, subscribe to Bitdefender Premium VPN, but you must do so at full price. VPN Premium isn’t included until you upgrade to the Bitdefender Premium Security package, which is much more expensive at $80 for the first year (and $130 per year after).

That could steer you away from Bitdefender if you want an antivirus and VPN. Competitors like Norton and McAfee bundle an unlimited VPN service into lower tiers of service, which, though more expensive than Bitdefender Antivirus Plus, are less expensive than Bitdefender Antivirus Plus and Bitdefender Premium VPN combined.

Unfortunate VPN bundling aside, Bitdefender Antivirus Plus is a good choice if you want antivirus for Windows, macOS, Android, and iOS.

It’s particularly competitive if you just want the antivirus protection on its own. All of the major cybersecurity companies would really prefer it if you bought a bundle (including Bitdefender), and they’re often good value if you actually use all the features. But there are plenty of reasons to just go for the antivirus itself (for example, credit and dark web monitoring features might already be available through your bank or another institution). And if antivirus protection is all you want, Bitdefender Antivirus Plus is a superb choice.

Bitdefender’s advantage is quite simple. It provides antivirus protection that detects most threats at a price that often undercuts the competition. It’s hard to think of a reason to pay more for an alternative when Bitdefender does the job well for as little as $25 for the first year.
