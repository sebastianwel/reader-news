[1] RESTAURANTS GEBEN REDUZIERTE MEHRWERTSTEUER KAUM WEITER

Im Januar wurde der Mehrwertsteuersatz auf Speisen in der Gastronomie reduziert. Eine neue Befragung zeigt, dass die große Mehrheit der Betriebe ihre Preise seitdem nicht gesenkt hat.

Im Restaurant von Amritvir Singh Dillon gibt es zwei klare Bestseller: das Butter Chicken und das Chicken Tikka Masala. Beide Gerichte kosten jeweils 18,90 Euro. Damit liege er im Vergleich zu anderen indischen Restaurants wohl eher im oberen Bereich, sagt der Gastronom aus dem nordrhein-westfälischen Wermelskirchen selbst.

Im Januar hatte er die Gelegenheit, die Preise für seine Gäste zu senken. Die Bundesregierung hatte die Mehrwertsteuer für Speisen in der Gastronomie zu Jahresbeginn von 19 auf 7 Prozent reduziert. Dillon hat sich aber gegen die Preissenkung entschieden, wie die meisten anderen Gastronomen in Deutschland auch.

19 Prozent auf alles? Das würde viele Waren verteuern. Doch Fuest hat auch eine Idee zur Sozialverträglichkeit.

Denn in den vergangenen Monaten hätten sich auch seine Kosten stark erhöht. "Die Fixkosten, die Einkaufskosten, die Personalkosten", so der Restaurantinhaber. Der gesetzliche Mindestlohn, den er seinen Service-Mitarbeitern zahlt, ist zu Jahresbeginn deutlich gestiegen.

Hinzu kämen Preissprünge bei Lebensmitteln. Gerade erlebe er das beim Rapsöl. Sein Lieferant habe ihm in der vergangenen Woche geraten, schnell noch einige Kanister zu bestellen, denn schon bald würden die Preise wieder erhöht.

Die anstehende Senkung der Mehrwertsteuer für die Gastronomie dürfte bei den meisten Gästen nicht ankommen.

Der Hotel- und Gaststättenverband DEHOGA hat etwa 2.000 Gastronomie-Betriebe in Deutschland zur Senkung der Mehrwertsteuer befragt. Demnach haben 73,4 Prozent von ihnen die Preise stabil gehalten. Wie viele der übrigen Gaststätten die Preise gesenkt und wie viele sie erhöht haben, gibt der Verband nicht an.

Preissenkungen dürften aber eher die Ausnahme gewesen sein. Laut Statistischem Bundesamt sind die Preise für eine Hauptspeise in der Gastronomie am Jahresanfang im Durchschnitt gestiegen. Der ermäßigte Mehrwertsteuersatz habe dazu beigetragen, dass Preiserhöhungen deutlich geringer ausgefallen seien, heißt es vom DEHOGA.

Die gestiegene Inflation trifft besonders die Hotelbranche hart.

Mike Henning aus Hagen ist einen anderen Weg gegangen, er hat seine Preise gesenkt. Henning betreibt ein Restaurant am Hengsteysee, dort hat er im Januar jedes Hauptgericht um einen Euro günstiger gemacht. Somit hat er die Steuersenkung teilweise an seine Gäste weitergegeben.

Seine Philosophie: Mehr Besucher durch niedrigere Preise. "Das Teuerste in der Gastronomie sind die Personalkosten", sagt Henning. "Die Kosten sind genauso hoch, ob die Person drei Tische bedient oder ob sie acht Tische bedient." Er versuche, seine Produkte so günstig wie möglich anzubieten, um so für eine höhere Auslastung des Personals zu sorgen.

Als die Mehrwertsteuer gesenkt wurde, hat Henning offensiv mit seinen Preissenkungen geworben. Offenbar mit Erfolg: "Wir haben zu Jahresbeginn bis zu 30 Prozent mehr Umsatz gehabt als im Jahr davor", so der Gastronom. Auch sein Personal profitiere davon: "Wenn das Lokal voll ist, bekommt der Kellner mehr Trinkgeld."

Für immer mehr Menschen wird das Essengehen zum Luxus - und Restaurants müssen schließen.

In Wermelskirchen zeigen die Besucher des indischen Restaurants von Amritvir Singh Dillon Verständnis dafür, dass er die Steuersenkung nicht an sie weitergegeben hat. Bisher habe sich noch niemand bei ihm beschwert, sagt der Restaurantinhaber.

Das Ehepaar Reifers gehört zu den Stammgästen des Lokals. Die Gastronomie sei durch Corona und andere Krisen sehr gebeutelt worden, sagt Stefan Reifers: "Man muss sie unterstützen." Seine Frau Astrid ergänzt: "Das Personal muss auch bezahlt werden, die arbeiten hart und gut."

Die Steuersenkung könnte immerhin dazu führen, dass Gastronom Dillon seine Preise auch in Zukunft stabil halten kann. Dadurch habe er eine Art Puffer, in den kommenden Jahren will er auf Preiserhöhungen verzichten.

[2] OPENAI PAUSIERT TEILS ENTWICKLUNG VON KI-MODELL WEGEN SICHERHEITSBEDENKEN

OpenAI hat Zweifel an einem seiner neuen Modelle - und pausiert teilweise die interne Entwicklung. Grund sind Sicherheitsbedenken durch "kritische" Fähigkeiten von "Astra". Cyberangriffe auf hochsichere Ziele könnten möglich sein.

Die KI-Firma OpenAI hat die interne Entwicklung seines neuen Modells "Astra" in Teilen vorübergehend gestoppt und dies mit möglichen "kritischen" Fähigkeiten im Bereich der Cybersicherheit begründet.

Nach den Sicherheitsrichtlinien von OpenAI gilt ein Modell als kritisch, wenn es eigenständig schwerwiegende, tatsächliche Software-Schwachstellen - sogenannte Zero-Day-Exploits - erkennen und ausnutzen oder komplexe Cyberangriffe auf hochsichere Ziele ausführen kann.

"Während wir dieses Modell weiter testen und bewerten, deuten unsere vorläufigen Auswertungen auf eine so starke Leistung hin, dass wir ein 'kritisches' Fähigkeitsniveau zum jetzigen Zeitpunkt nicht ausschließen können", erklärte der ChatGPT-Entwickler. Als Reaktion darauf seien die Sicherheitskontrollen verschärft und die Entwicklung von "Astra" in isolierte Testumgebungen mit eingeschränktem Netzwerkzugang verlagert worden.

Wie groß ist die Gefahr für private Rechner und unsere Daten von KI-Alleingängen?

Der Fall verdeutlicht, wie zunehmend schwierig es für Entwickler wird, fortschrittliche Künstliche Intelligenz (KI) unter Kontrolle zu halten. In den vergangenen Wochen hatten OpenAI, Anthropic und Meta eingeräumt, dass ihre Modelle bei Sicherheitstests in die Systeme anderer Unternehmen eingedrungen waren.

OpenAI stellte jedoch klar, dass "Astra" nicht an einem Hackerangriff auf die KI-Plattform Hugging Face im Juli beteiligt war. Die Nachrichtenagentur Reuters hatte gemeldet, dass OpenAI bei der Untersuchung dieses Vorfalls weitere Fälle entdeckt habe, in denen autonome KI-Agenten aus ihren isolierten Umgebungen ausgebrochen seien.

Die Ankündigung erfolgte wenige Tage nach einer aufsehenerregenden Testpanne bei OpenAI.

Nach den Hackerangriffen haben fast 40 US-Technologieunternehmen eine Sicherheitsallianz gegründet. Dem Bündnis namens Open Secure AI Alliance gehören Konzerne wie IBM, Nvidia, Microsoft und Palantir an. Auch die angegriffene Plattform Hugging Face ist beteiligt. Sie setzen zur Lösung des Problems ebenfalls auf KI.

Konkret wollen die Unternehmen sogenannte Open-Source-Modelle Künstlicher Intelligenz zur Cyberabwehr bereitstellen. Programmierer sollen diese dann weiterentwickeln.

[1] THE CHINESE PHILOSOPHER AMERICANS CAN’T STOP FIGHTING ABOUT

Yiyang Zhuge learned last week that when you go extremely viral, the people you have been following for years suddenly start following you back. Zhuge logged onto X and found that a string of famous professors and scholars were in her list of new followers. A PhD candidate in political philosophy at Boston College, Zhuge had previously only lurked on X to keep up with the latest debates and gossip in academia. The vast majority of her social media activity happens on the Chinese internet, where she goes by the alias Zhong Shu and hosts a popular podcast with hundreds of thousands of loyal listeners.

Late last month, Zhuge, who is in her late twenties, interviewed Christopher Nolan, one of the most influential directors in Hollywood, who was in Beijing promoting The Odyssey. Rather than asking about celebrities or Hollywood, Zhuge posed a series of deeply philosophical questions to Nolan about the Christianization of Greek philosophy and how to portray heroes in films. The first thing she asked: “Are you a bard for a civilization at dusk?”

Nolan was visibly impressed by Zhuge. After the interview was published in China, Geoff Keighley, a video game journalist and the son of Nolan’s longtime friend David Keighley, shared the interview on X, calling it “a great listen.” Keighley’s video has been watched over 18 million times, and when Zhuge later posted the interview on her own X account, it racked up another 6.9 million views.

For the first time, Zhuge had become the main character of the English-speaking internet. She was being praised, mocked, criticized, and defended all at the same time. Many people said it was the best interview Nolan has done for The Odyssey, while the National Review called it a “CCP hostage video.” But everyone ultimately wanted to know the same thing: Who was this young Chinese woman with a bob haircut talking about the decline of civilizations?

While almost no one in the West knows her name, Zhuge is already one of the most prominent young intellectuals in China and has been interviewed by many of the country’s cultural elite. She was featured in Vogue China as an example of a successful woman in her twenties and frequently shares yoga and fitness tips to her 360,000 fans on the Chinese social media platform Xiaohongshu.

When I spoke to Zhuge this week, she framed her success as being born out of personal tragedy. “I was going through an adulterous divorce in which my ex-husband sued me and I accumulated a lot of legal debt,” she says. “I felt discouraged in the English-speaking academia because of certain personal experiences related to that personal tragedy, so I turned my attention back to the Chinese-speaking world to build a career there and pay off my debt.”

When I started digging into Zhuge’s career, I was shocked by her level of productivity. Aside from publishing philosophy translations and writing articles for Chinese media outlets, she has recorded more than 360 episodes of her podcast Monologues of a Committed Observer in just the last three years. That’s an average of one new episode every three days. Most of the time, she just monologues about political science concepts for a half an hour, seemingly unscripted. On XiaoyuzhouFM, the most popular podcast streaming platform in China, she has over 940,000 subscribers.

But despite her prolific posting, Zhuge claims she isn’t interested in being famous. “I have no attachment to fame and no desire to preserve it for its own sake,” she tells me. “What I want is enough freedom and time to build a life centered on thought. That life requires some money, but not very much.”

When Zhuge asked Nolan if he saw himself as “a bard for a civilization at dusk,” she says she didn’t have a particular civilization in mind.

She views the question instead as part of the long philosophical tradition of pondering the demise of value systems. “I was not primarily attempting to issue a verdict on the contemporary United States,” she says, but “people are free to interpret the phrase through current debates about American or Western decline.”

That’s certainly how many people on Western social media interpreted it. On X, YouTube, and Reddit, commenters lamented about how Western interviewers are seemingly unable to ask the same kind of rigorous questions to their subjects. Some wondered why a Chinese woman was the one to ask philosophical questions about Greece, while others asked if Western institutions are still teaching students humanities and the classics.

To be fair, Zhuge was educated in the Western system. She has been studying in the US since high school, lectured at Boston College and Brandeis University, and translated books by Hannah Arendt and Plutarch (an ancient Greek philosopher) into Chinese. She lives in Boston for most of the year. Her academic work draws on Straussianism, an influential school of conservative political philosophy that has shaped the thinking of people like Peter Thiel. Her fame in China, which earned her the opportunity to talk to Nolan in the first place, is mostly tied to the way she can succinctly explain the American political system, though some critics think her own conservative ideological leanings show up often in her work.

“She was trained in American conservatism, so the questions she asked really resonated with the US’s educated public,” says Chenchen Zhang, an associate professor in international relations at Durham University. When the familiar insecurities of American conservatives came out of the mouth of a Chinese woman, the audience instantly believed “this is how China views the Western civilization,” Zhang says.

The internet’s reaction to Zhuge’s interview reminds me of “Chinamaxxing,” the meme that persisted through the first half of 2026 on platforms like Instagram and TikTok. The trend was promoted mostly by left-leaning, Gen-Z Americans trying to numb their anxiety about America’s decline by deliberately endorsing China and Chinese culture. Now, right-leaning Americans are indulging in the same kinds of worries by fawning over Zhuge’s reverence for the classics. They are really two sides of the same coin.

As the American right rushed to claim Zhuge as one of its own, the far left moved to distance itself from her. On X, @zei_squirrel, a prominent but anonymous progressive account with 346,000 followers, called her either “a deranged fanatical neocon Zionist lunatic” or a Chinese state-media psy-op, pointing to the fact that Zhuge follows several conservative figures.

Zhuge says she’s no stranger to people across the political spectrum being mad at her. “In China, I try very hard to remain above the fray of the mob rather than let public controversy determine what I think or discuss.” she says. “The labels may differ across countries, but the vulgar human impulse to sort people into political camps is universal.”

As for the anonymous account’s claim that she is a Zionist, Zhuge posted one rebuttal on X but says she won’t engage further. “An account that searches through someone’s following list for Jewish names is not conducting any meaningful analysis,” she says, “This mentality is one reason American online public life has become so polarized. People no longer examine arguments; they investigate affiliations.”

Still, this brief moment of virality has clearly shaken her. In her most recent podcast episode published on Thursday, Zhuge talked about how this was the first time she truly felt that she became the subject of public discourse. There’s a not-so-subtle hint of rage in her tone, and the audience seemed to notice it too. One commenter noted that she had forgotten to say her signature farewell message at the end. Instead, she ended the podcast episode by playing Beethoven’s Symphony No. 3, which the composer dedicated to heroes. “I would rather listen to how revolution collided with destiny in the ‘Eroica’ Symphony than a bunch of people in my notifications speculating who was my puppeteer pulling the strings,” she said.

[2] THE 7 BEST TV SHOWS TO STREAM THIS MONTH

The big screen is having a moment. Spider-Man: Brand New Day is breaking box office records, while Christopher Nolan’s The Odyssey has people dusting off Homer for the first time since high school. But television is keeping pace with a slate of can’t-miss new releases and beloved old favorites that are worth revisiting.

While plenty of outlets will tell you what everyone is watching (see: Ted Lasso), that’s not our goal. As always, the TV shows we’re watching this month reflect the kinds of stories WIRED covers every day—shows that explore technology, science, politics, culture, and the sometimes unsettling ways they intersect.

From prestige dramas to absurd comedies and one truly groundbreaking sci-fi series, these are the TV shows we think are worth making time for this month.

On September 8, 1966, science fiction changed forever with the premiere of the original Star Trek TV series. In the 60 years since, Gene Roddenberry’s ahead-of-its-time tale of the USS Enterprise crew’s journeys into deep space has spawned dozens of films, TV series, and video games, plus hundreds of books and comics—not to mention a number of real-world tech innovations. The legendary series has also shaped pop culture in countless other ways, giving us everything from Vulcan salutes and Klingons to the Prime Directive and the immortal command to “beam me up.”

Whether you’re a lifelong Trekkie or a curious newcomer, there’s never been a better time to see where one of television’s most influential franchises began. Though it ran for just three seasons, Star Trek: The Original Series left an outsized legacy, breaking barriers with one of television’s first interracial kisses, featuring a remarkably diverse cast at the height of the Civil Rights Movement, and inspiring generations of scientists, engineers, and storytellers to imagine a more optimistic future. Sixty years later, its blend of adventure, social commentary, and boundless curiosity remains as compelling as ever. Best of all, Paramount+ is home to nearly every corner of the Star Trek universe, from the beautifully remastered original series to its many spinoffs and feature films. If you’re celebrating the franchise’s 60th anniversary, there’s no better place to boldly go back to where it all began.

Following a decade-long hiatus, Futurama made a triumphant comeback in 2023, complete with gags about The Twilight Zone and “Momazon” drone deliveries. Created by Matt Groening and David X. Cohen, the animated sci-fi comedy is set in the 31st century and follows the employees of the Planet Express delivery company as they stumble through bizarre adventures, alien encounters, and laugh-out-loud takes on everything from AI to corporate greed.

On August 3, the series kicked off its 14th season, which delivers more of the razor-sharp tech satire and surprisingly thoughtful science that have long made it one of television’s smartest comedies.

This summer is a particularly ripe moment for fans of irreverent animated sci-fi, as it also brings President Curtis, a new spinoff set in the Rick and Morty universe. While Futurama looks ahead at the possibilities—and absurdities—of the distant future, President Curtis takes a stranger route, following President Andre Curtis (voiced by Emmy-winning baritone Keith David) and his eccentric staff as they navigate inter-dimensional diplomacy, paranormal investigations, and other bizarre crises. The 10-episode series, which premiered on July 26, airs new episodes each Sunday night on Adult Swim, which then drop on HBO Max on Monday.

DC is taking its Green Lanterns in a decidedly darker direction with Lanterns, a new series that trades traditional superhero spectacle for a cosmic murder mystery. Starring Kyle Chandler as Hal Jordan and Aaron Pierre as John Stewart, the series follows the two Lanterns as they investigate a mystery that stretches across Earth and beyond.

With Lost cocreator Damon Lindelof, True Detective producer Chris Mundy, and comic book writer Tom King behind the series, Lanterns—which arrives on August 16—brings a noir-inspired edge to one of DC’s most iconic franchises.

In the early morning hours of November 13, 2022, the city of Moscow, Idaho, changed forever when four University of Idaho students were killed in their off-campus home. The investigation that followed captivated the nation, as authorities worked to piece together what happened and identify a suspect in a horrifying quadruple murder that appeared to have no clear motive.

The Idaho Murders: College Nightmare chronicles the investigation through access to victims’ friends and families and police footage, while examining how digital evidence and forensic technology helped investigators unravel one of the most closely watched true crime cases in recent years.

What if the life you’re living isn’t the one you were meant to have? That’s the question at the heart of Dark Matter, the sci-fi thriller based on Blake Crouch’s best-selling novel. Season one followed physicist Jason Dessen (Joel Edgerton), who is pulled into an alternate version of his life after being transported to a parallel reality.

Season two, which premieres on August 28, picks up with Dessen and his family attempting to build a quiet existence in a world that finally feels safe … until new threats force them back into the multiverse with the help of a mysterious device known as the Box. As the search for the Box continues, the series dives deeper into questions of identity, choice, and the endless possibilities of the paths we could have taken.

Four years after its debut, The Undeclared War is back with a chillingly timely premise: a cyberattack targeting a nation’s election system. The British thriller returns to the shadowy world of GCHQ (the UK’s intelligence, security, and cyber agency), where analysts battle digital threats that could have devastating real-world consequences.

Set in a post-pandemic 2024, the series follows a team of analysts racing to defend the country’s digital infrastructure as tensions with Russia escalate. With Shaun of the Dead star Simon Pegg starring as operations chief Danny Patrick, the new season, which arrives on August 27, explores how cyberwarfare has transformed the nature of modern espionage.

[3] ZOHRAN MAMDANI’S NYC TECH TEAM IS WHAT DOGE SHOULD HAVE BEEN

A go-kart track is an odd location to launch a tech initiative in the nation’s biggest city. Yet last month there was New York City mayor Zohran Mamdani, zipping around the 900-foot oval at Coney Island’s Luna Park before stepping up to a podium to unveil a program called Public Interest Technology (PIT) Crews. Thus the go-kart theme.

PIT will consist of five “game-changing” teams that, Mamdani promised, will “raise the bar for what New Yorkers can expect from City Hall.” Working closely with city agencies, these small groups of engineers and designers will strive to change the hidebound and confusing tenor of current city services by using state-of-art skills to rapidly whip up specialized apps that solve real problems. “We want to transform how New Yorkers interact with the government,” said the mayor. “We want to raise expectations on what government can deliver, because we really can deliver.”

Do those words sound familiar? If you follow government tech, they might. Because Mamdani’s message could have fit quite comfortably in the pitch that Barack Obama’s chief technology officer, Todd Park, delivered in 2014 while recruiting tech talent for what would become the United States Digital Service. The USDS was an idealistic effort to bring top Silicon Valley talent into the executive branch to bypass the logjams caused by outdated and inefficient IT, by building great, user-centric software.

The agency somehow survived the first Trump administration and kept going through the Biden term. But in 2025, Elon Musk and his DOGE wrecking crew infiltrated the agency and pulled the plug on much of the useful stuff. (Now, with the National Design Service, the Trump administration is ostensibly trying to revive some of what it destroyed.)

Mamdani’s PIT crew initiative adopts much of the original USDS ethos, with a timely twist: It embraces Silicon Valley expertise while taking a skeptical, almost adversarial, stance toward Big Tech itself. The combination of mayoral charisma and a chance to make software that doesn’t serve advertisers, the military, or the pocketbooks of centibillionaires makes the Mamdani team an attractive, high-status, mid-career change of pace for some techies. Suddenly, one of the sexiest places in geekdom is the Brooklyn headquarters of New York City’s Office of Technology and Innovation.

Mamdani’s transition team set the stage for an urban, democratic-socialist-adjacent, USDS-style tech squad when it tapped Lisa Gelobter for the city’s chief technology officer job. Gelobter’s résumé includes stints at big companies and startups, but the standout item was her post at the United States Digital Service, where she was embedded in the Department of Education. She led the effort to create a College Scorecard that focused on nuts-and-bolts criteria like costs and graduation rates. To this day, she gets misty when recalling her Washington experience; she even remembers what she was wearing when the USDS team took a group picture with Obama just before he left office.

“I’m really excited about trying to recapture that essence, that energy here,” she says. Her budget for the program is $5.24 million, with an additional $2 million grant from the Rockefeller Foundation, which will fund one of the crews.

Gelobter says she brings an engineering mindset to New York City government. “I know how to build software,” she says. “I know how to run a technology organization from a technology perspective.” It’s also a cultural thing for her. “I’m wearing jeans to work—not because I don’t look great in a suit, but it’s a statement, right?” she says. (The same norm-breaking happened in the USDS, which had to overcome objections from bureaucrats who didn’t want to meet with anyone wearing a hoodie.) Naturally, the two people Gelobter hired to run the PIT crew program are also USDS veterans, Luke Farrell and Maya Israni.

The projects Gelobter and her team choose to tackle will set a tone for the way the government works under a mayor whose future depends on how well he manages his city. “Without technology, policy is just words written on a piece of paper,” says Gelobter. Seen that way, the PIT crews are essentially Mamdani’s symbolic stand-ins in his fight against self-interested consultants and inefficient contractors. They will make their case by actually building things, in frantic 12-week sprints.

On its face, the first project of the PIT crew seems nonintuitive. The mission is to implement a pet mayoral project: click-to-cancel. On July 10, Mamdani announced this regulation, which penalizes companies that make it difficult to cancel subscriptions or ongoing payments. The rule takes effect on October 1, which is the exact date that the PIT crew plans to launch software that empowers citizens to blow the whistle on services that force people to go through hoops to end the relationship. (Mamdami might be the first customer; on Coney Island he admitted, “I am currently subscribed to something I don’t even know my login for, but they have my credit card information, and I don’t know how to cancel it.”)

The city hasn’t yet announced what the other four PIT crews will work on. My guess is that they will concentrate on more nuts-and-boltsy city services, and at least one will address the housing problem—but it’s telling that this first choice provides a way for ordinary people to stand up to bullying corporations. “It will show that we can directly affect people’s lives. It’s supposed to save, like, $160 million for New Yorkers,” says Gelobter. The project, she confirms, was a “mayoral priority.”

It’s perfect Mamdani branding. Even though much of Hizzoner’s personal tech surge is based on the USDS playbook, the conditions in 2026 are considerably different than they were in 2014. Back then, most people saw Silicon Valley as an enviable font of innovation, gumption, and high standards. The Obama-ites wanted to reflect Silicon Valley’s glory. These days, Big Tech is seen as an oppressive force, especially by progressive politicians and young people. So the choice sends a message that Mamdani’s version of a geek squad can not only make services accessible, but could also check the impulses of oligarchs.

That message seems to be resonating among techies who want to do something other than develop AI that might decimate the labor force. Farrell says that in the first week after the city posted PIT crew job opportunities, over 3,000 people applied for the roughly 35 available slots, and résumés are still pouring in. A shocking number have come from people who currently hold high-salaried jobs in big tech companies. (The city jobs pay considerably less, with no equity.)

“We've heard from a lot of folks recently who are disillusioned with the private tech industry and maybe went into tech because they wanted to apply tech for good,” says Israni. If those people make it to PIT crews, they can indeed apply their skills for good. Or, at the least, for Mamdani.
